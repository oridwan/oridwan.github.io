Space-group symmetry

An introduction to crystallographic symmetry built around figures you can manipulate. Each section introduces one idea; the last one links to a page for every space group, where its operations, symmetry elements and Wyckoff positions are shown step by step in three dimensions.

Data follow the standard settings of the International Tables for Crystallography, Vol. A, generated with PyXtal and spglib. Every group page links to the Bilbao Crystallographic Server for comparison.

1Lattice and motif

A crystal is a periodic arrangement: one unit, the motif, repeated by a lattice of translations. Three non-coplanar translation vectors a, b, c span the unit cell, and any point is written in fractional coordinates (x, y, z):

r = x a + y b + z c

Adding a whole number to a coordinate moves the point to the same place in a neighbouring cell. Coordinates are therefore usually reduced to 0 ≤ x, y, z < 1. The figure shows the two-dimensional case.

2Symmetry operations

A symmetry operation moves the whole pattern onto itself. Lattice translations are always among them. The others are rotations, reflections and their combinations, and the geometric object that stays fixed (an axis, a plane or a point) is called the symmetry element.

2.1 In the plane

In two dimensions the possibilities are rotations, reflections in a line, and glide reflections (reflection followed by a translation of half a lattice repeat along the line). Rotations are limited to 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold. A 5-fold or 7-fold rotation cannot be combined with a lattice, a result known as the crystallographic restriction.

2.2 In three dimensions

Two new kinds of operation appear in three dimensions: inversion through a point, and rotoinversion, a rotation followed by inversion. A rotation may also be coupled to a translation along its axis (a screw rotation), and a reflection to a translation parallel to its plane (a glide reflection). Because of their translation part, screw and glide operations leave no point fixed.

3Crystal systems

Rotational symmetry constrains the shape of the unit cell. A 4-fold axis along c, for instance, forces a = b and a right angle between them. Grouping crystals by the symmetry they must have gives seven crystal systems. The system is defined by the symmetry, not by the cell shape alone: a cell can be metrically cubic by accident and still be tetragonal.

4Centring and the 14 Bravais lattices

The conventional cell is chosen so that its edges follow the symmetry directions. Sometimes such a cell contains more than one lattice point, and the extra points sit at its centre or on its faces. The centring is given by a letter: P (primitive), A, B or C (one pair of faces centred), I (body-centred), F (all faces centred) and R (rhombohedral, in hexagonal axes).

Combining centrings with crystal systems gives exactly 14 distinct Bravais lattices. Other combinations are not new: a C-centred tetragonal lattice, for example, is the same as a smaller primitive tetragonal one.

5From point groups to space groups

If the translations are ignored, the operations of a crystal reduce to rotations, reflections, inversion and rotoinversions about a single point. These form one of the 32 crystallographic point groups (crystal classes), which govern macroscopic properties such as optical activity, piezoelectricity and the symmetry of the diffraction pattern.

A space group combines a Bravais lattice with a point group, and allows each rotation or reflection to carry a fractional translation. When that translation cannot be removed by moving the origin, it produces a screw axis or glide plane. Every operation is written as a coordinate triplet. In P21/c, for example,

(x, y, z) → (−x, y + ½, −z + ½)

is a 2-fold rotation about the line (0, y, ¼) combined with a translation of ½ along b: a 21 screw axis. In matrix form, an operation is a pair (W, w) acting as x′ = Wx + w. W is the rotation part and w the translation part.

6Reading Hermann–Mauguin symbols

A space-group symbol is the lattice letter followed by up to three entries. Each entry describes the symmetry belonging to one set of directions: a rotation or screw axis along the direction, a plane perpendicular to it, or both, written as a fraction (2/m, 42/n). 7Wyckoff positions and site symmetry

Applying all operations of a group to a point in general position produces the maximum number of copies. If the point lies on a symmetry element that leaves points fixed (a rotation axis, a mirror plane or an inversion centre), some operations map it onto itself and copies coincide. The point is then in a special position.

Each family of equivalent positions is a Wyckoff position, labelled by its multiplicity (the number of copies per cell) and a Wyckoff letter, from a for the most special up to the general position. The operations that leave a point fixed form its site-symmetry group, and

multiplicity × order of the site-symmetry group = multiplicity of the general position.

The plane group p2mm shows every case. Drag the point and watch the copies merge on the mirror lines and at their intersections. Near a symmetry element the point snaps onto it.

Tables such as the Bilbao server's WYCKPOS list the same four columns for every space group: multiplicity, letter, site symmetry and coordinate triplets. For centred lattices, the triplets are preceded by the centring vectors, e.g. (0, 0, 0)+ (½, ½, 0)+, meaning each listed position is repeated at each centring translation.
