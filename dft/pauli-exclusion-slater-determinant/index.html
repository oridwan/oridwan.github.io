DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 51 / 15

Lectures 2 to 4 were mostly about one electron at a time. Real atoms, molecules and solids have many, and they repel each other and obey a rule that has no classical counterpart: two electrons can never be in the same spin-orbital. This lecture shows how much the repulsion matters, where the Pauli principle comes from, how a Slater determinant builds it in, and why nobody stores the full many-electron wavefunction of a solid.

How much does electron repulsion matter?

Ignore it and helium's energy is 30 eV wrong. A covalent bond is worth about 5 eV.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 52 / 15

Helium has two electrons and a nucleus of charge Z=2Z = 2Z=2. Drop the repulsion between the electrons and the Hamiltonian splits into two hydrogen-like problems. Each electron sits in a 1s orbital with energy −Z2/2-Z^2/2−Z2/2 hartree, so the total is −Z2=−4-Z^2 = -4−Z2=−4 hartree, or −108.8-108.8−108.8 eV.

Experiment says otherwise. The first electron takes 24.6 eV to remove and the second 54.4 eV, so the true ground-state energy is −79.0-79.0−79.0 eV. The non-interacting picture is off by 30 eV. For scale, breaking one O–H bond in water takes about 5.1 eV, and a typical strong covalent bond is worth about 5 eV: the error is six bonds' worth.

Adding the repulsion back as an average over the non-interacting wavefunction costs +5Z8+\tfrac{5Z}{8}+85Z​ hartree =+34= +34=+34 eV and gives −74.8-74.8−74.8 eV, only 4 eV off. Those last 4 eV are still close to a whole covalent bond's worth, and closing that gap is what the rest of Part I is about.

Before dealing with that, the product picture has a second problem, which has nothing to do with energy.

What is electron spin?

A fourth coordinate with two values, up or down; an orbital times a spin is a spin-orbital.

  • Two valuesms=+12m_s = +\tfrac12ms​=+21​ (α, “up”) or ms=−12m_s = -\tfrac12ms​=−21​ (β, “down”).

  • CoordinatesEach electron: xi=(ri,si)\mathbf x_i = (\mathbf r_i, s_i)xi​=(ri​,si​), position plus spin.

  • One spatial orbital, two spin-orbitals.

Electrons carry an intrinsic angular momentum, spin, whose component along any chosen axis takes only two values, ms=±12m_s = \pm\tfrac12ms​=±21​ (in units of ℏ\hbarℏ). It shows up in experiment: in 1922 Stern and Gerlach sent a beam of silver atoms through an inhomogeneous magnetic field and saw it split in two, a splitting later understood as the spin of the atom's one unpaired electron.

To include spin in a wavefunction, give each electron a fourth coordinate sss that takes two values, and write x=(r,s)\mathbf x = (\mathbf r, s)x=(r,s) for position plus spin. The two spin functions, α(s)\alpha(s)α(s) for up and β(s)\beta(s)β(s) for down, are orthonormal: summing over the two values of sss, ⟨α∣α⟩=⟨β∣β⟩=1\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle = \langle\beta|\beta\rangle = 1⟨α∣α⟩=⟨β∣β⟩=1 and ⟨α∣β⟩=0\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle = 0⟨α∣β⟩=0. A product of a spatial orbital and a spin function is a spin-orbital, and two spin-orbitals with different spins are orthogonal even if their spatial parts are the same.

The Hamiltonian of Lecture 1 does not act on spin at all; spin enters only through the symmetry rule on the next slides. (Spin–orbit coupling, which does act on it, is the subject of Lecture 24.)

What does “identical” mean for electrons?

Swapping the labels of two electrons cannot change any measurement, so Ψ can at most change sign.

∣Ψ(x2,x1)∣2=∣Ψ(x1,x2)∣2⟹Ψ(x2,x1)=± Ψ(x1,x2)|\Psi(\mathbf x_2, \mathbf x_1)|^2 = |\Psi(\mathbf x_1, \mathbf x_2)|^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Psi(\mathbf x_2, \mathbf x_1) = \eqterm{pm}{\pm}\, \Psi(\mathbf x_1, \mathbf x_2)∣Ψ(x2​,x1​)∣2=∣Ψ(x1​,x2​)∣2⟹Ψ(x2​,x1​)=±Ψ(x1​,x2​)
  • The sign: swapping twice must give back Ψ, so the factor squares to 1. Nature fixes it: +++ for bosons (photons), −-− for fermions (electrons).

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 55 / 15

Two electrons cannot be told apart. There is no “electron 1” and “electron 2” in nature, only labels we attach for bookkeeping, and swapping the labels cannot change the outcome of any measurement. So every probability must be unchanged by the swap, which means the wavefunction can change at most by a factor of magnitude one.

Write P^12\hat P_{12}P^12​ for the operator that swaps the labels, P^12Ψ(x1,x2)=Ψ(x2,x1)\hat P_{12}\Psi(\mathbf x_1, \mathbf x_2) = \Psi(\mathbf x_2, \mathbf x_1)P^12​Ψ(x1​,x2​)=Ψ(x2​,x1​). Swapping twice restores the original, P^122=1\hat P_{12}^2 = 1P^122​=1, so the eigenvalues of P^12\hat P_{12}P^12​ are +1+1+1 and −1-1−1: an acceptable wavefunction is either symmetric or antisymmetric under the swap. The Hamiltonian treats all electrons alike, so it commutes with P^12\hat P_{12}P^12​, and a state keeps its symmetry forever. Which sign electrons take is not a choice we make; the next slide gives nature's answer.

Which sign do electrons take?

The minus sign: electron wavefunctions are antisymmetric, and same-spin electrons never meet.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 56 / 15

Particles with half-integer spin, fermions such as electrons, protons and neutrons, have antisymmetric wavefunctions; particles with integer spin, bosons such as photons, have symmetric ones. This is the spin–statistics theorem. Its proof belongs to relativistic quantum field theory, so in this course we take it as a postulate.

The simple product of spin-orbitals fails the test, because swapping the labels gives a different function. The fix is to subtract the swapped product:

Ψ=12[χa(x1)χb(x2)−χb(x1)χa(x2)].\Psi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big[\chi_a(\mathbf x_1)\chi_b(\mathbf x_2) - \chi_b(\mathbf x_1)\chi_a(\mathbf x_2)\big].Ψ=2​1​[χa​(x1​)χb​(x2​)−χb​(x1​)χa​(x2​)].

Swapping 1 and 2 turns the bracket into its negative, as required. When the two spin-orbitals have the same spin, the spin factor α(s1)α(s2)\alpha(s_1)\alpha(s_2)α(s1​)α(s2​) comes out, and the spatial part is [ϕa(r1)ϕb(r2)−ϕb(r1)ϕa(r2)]/2[\phi_a(\mathbf r_1)\phi_b(\mathbf r_2) - \phi_b(\mathbf r_1)\phi_a(\mathbf r_2)]/\sqrt2[ϕa​(r1​)ϕb​(r2​)−ϕb​(r1​)ϕa​(r2​)]/2​. That is what the figure shows, with ϕa\phi_aϕa​ and ϕb\phi_bϕb​ the box states ψ1\psi_1ψ1​ and ψ2\psi_2ψ2​.

On the diagonal x1=x2x_1 = x_2x1​=x2​ the two terms cancel, so the antisymmetric ∣Ψ∣2|\Psi|^2∣Ψ∣2 vanishes there: two electrons of the same spin are never found at the same place. The probability of finding them within a tenth of the box of each other is 0.016, against 0.200 for the plain product, more than ten times smaller. That region of reduced probability around each electron is the exchange hole. The symmetric combination does the opposite and piles the electrons up (0.385).

A general rule follows for two electrons. The total wavefunction is a spatial part times a spin part, and the product must be antisymmetric. So either the spatial part is antisymmetric and the spin part symmetric (the three triplet states, which include both spins up), or the spatial part is symmetric and the spin part antisymmetric (the singlet, [α(1)β(2)−β(1)α(2)]/2[\alpha(1)\beta(2) - \beta(1)\alpha(2)]/\sqrt2[α(1)β(2)−β(1)α(2)]/2​). Helium's ground state is the singlet: both electrons in 1s with opposite spins, spatial part 1s(r1) 1s(r2)1s(\mathbf r_1)\,1s(\mathbf r_2)1s(r1​)1s(r2​), the same product as before. That is why antisymmetry does not change helium's estimates on the first slide; exchange acts between electrons of the same spin, and its effects show up as soon as two electrons share one.

How do you build an antisymmetric wavefunction?

Write the spin-orbitals as a determinant: swapping two electrons swaps two rows and flips the sign.

Ψ(x1,…,xN)=1N!∣χ1(x1)χ2(x1)⋯χN(x1)χ1(x2)χ2(x2)⋯χN(x2)⋮⋱⋮χ1(xN)χ2(xN)⋯χN(xN)∣\Psi(\mathbf x_1, \dots, \mathbf x_N) = \eqterm{norm}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}} \eqterm{det}{\begin{vmatrix} \chi_1(\mathbf x_1) & \chi_2(\mathbf x_1) & \cdots & \chi_N(\mathbf x_1) \\ \chi_1(\mathbf x_2) & \chi_2(\mathbf x_2) & \cdots & \chi_N(\mathbf x_2) \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ \chi_1(\mathbf x_N) & \chi_2(\mathbf x_N) & \cdots & \chi_N(\mathbf x_N) \end{vmatrix}}Ψ(x1​,…,xN​)=N!​1​​χ1​(x1​)χ1​(x2​)⋮χ1​(xN​)​χ2​(x1​)χ2​(x2​)χ2​(xN​)​⋯⋯⋱⋯​χN​(x1​)χN​(x2​)⋮χN​(xN​)​​
  • Rows are electrons, columns are spin-orbitals. Swapping two electrons swaps two rows, which flips the sign of any determinant.
  • Normalization for orthonormal spin-orbitals; for N=2N = 2N=2, the 1/21/\sqrt21/2​ of the previous slide.

For more than two electrons, writing out all the signed products by hand quickly becomes impossible: three electrons already need six terms, ten electrons over three million. In 1929 John Slater found a compact solution in the properties of determinants. Put the spin-orbitals in the columns and the electrons in the rows. For two electrons,

12∣χa(x1)χb(x1)χa(x2)χb(x2)∣=12[χa(x1)χb(x2)−χb(x1)χa(x2)],\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{vmatrix} \chi_a(\mathbf x_1) & \chi_b(\mathbf x_1) \\ \chi_a(\mathbf x_2) & \chi_b(\mathbf x_2) \end{vmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big[\chi_a(\mathbf x_1)\chi_b(\mathbf x_2) - \chi_b(\mathbf x_1)\chi_a(\mathbf x_2)\big],2​1​​χa​(x1​)χa​(x2​)​χb​(x1​)χb​(x2​)​​=2​1​[χa​(x1​)χb​(x2​)−χb​(x1​)χa​(x2​)],

exactly the wavefunction of the previous slide. For N electrons, expanding the determinant produces all N!N!N! assignments of electrons to orbitals, each with the sign of its permutation. Two properties of determinants then do all the work: swapping two rows flips the sign, which is antisymmetry; and two equal columns give zero, which is the next slide.

A Slater determinant is the simplest antisymmetric many-electron wavefunction. Each electron is still in its own orbital, but the electrons can no longer be told which. It is the starting point of Hartree–Fock theory (Lecture 6) and of the Kohn–Sham construction (Lecture 9).

Where does the Pauli exclusion principle come from?

A determinant with two equal columns is zero: no two electrons can occupy the same spin-orbital.

∣χa(x1)χa(x1)χa(x2)χa(x2)∣=0\begin{vmatrix} \chi_a(\mathbf x_1) & \chi_a(\mathbf x_1) \\ \chi_a(\mathbf x_2) & \chi_a(\mathbf x_2) \end{vmatrix} = 0​χa​(x1​)χa​(x2​)​χa​(x1​)χa​(x2​)​​=0
  • Two electrons in the same spin-orbital: the wavefunction vanishes everywhere.
  • Pauli exclusionAt most one electron per spin-orbital, so at most two per spatial orbital, with opposite spins.
  • ConsequenceShells fill in order: the aufbau rule of Lecture 4.
  • In VASPOccupation 2 per band without spin polarization; 1 per band per spin with it.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 58 / 15

A determinant with two identical columns is zero. Two identical columns would mean two electrons in the same spin-orbital, so such a wavefunction does not exist: this is the Pauli exclusion principle. Pauli proposed it in 1925 as a rule to explain atomic spectra; antisymmetry shows that it is not a separate law but a consequence of electrons being identical fermions. From it the aufbau filling order of Lecture 4 follows.

Since spin-orbitals with different spins are different, each spatial orbital can hold two electrons, one with each spin. That is why VASP's OUTCAR shows occupations of 2 for the filled bands of Lab 0, and why a spin-polarized run (ISPIN = 2), which treats up and down electrons in separate channels, shows occupations of 1 in each channel.

Is one Slater determinant the exact answer?

No. It enforces antisymmetry, but electrons also avoid each other because they repel: correlation.

  • Built in by a determinantExchange: same-spin electrons keep apart.
  • Missing from one determinantCorrelation: all electrons, of either spin, avoiding each other because of 1/∣ri−rj∣1/|\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_j|1/∣ri​−rj​∣.
  • HeliumFirst estimate −74.8 eV, measured −79.0 eV. Letting the orbital relax (Lecture 6) gives −77.9 eV; the last 1.1 eV is correlation.

A Slater determinant makes the wavefunction antisymmetric, and with it same-spin electrons avoid each other. But a determinant is still built from independent orbitals: for an electron of the other spin, where it is remains uncorrelated with where you are. Real electrons repel, so all of them avoid each other more than a single determinant allows. The missing piece is called correlation.

Some states cannot even be written as a single determinant. Helium's excited configuration 1s2s with one electron up and one down is an example: its singlet state is [1s(1)2s(2)+2s(1)1s(2)][1s(1)2s(2) + 2s(1)1s(2)][1s(1)2s(2)+2s(1)1s(2)] times the singlet spin function, and expanding that product gives two determinants, not one. Lecture 6 finds the best single determinant, the Hartree–Fock wavefunction, and defines the correlation energy as what it leaves out: for helium, the last 1.1 eV.

Why does no code store the many-electron Ψ of a solid?

On a grid of g points per coordinate, Ψ needs g³ᴺ numbers; the density needs g³, whatever N is.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 510 / 15

Lecture 0 met the exponential wall as a count of parameters. Here is the same wall as storage. Put each coordinate on a grid of only g=10g = 10g=10 points. One electron's orbital then takes 10310^3103 numbers. The many-electron wavefunction of the 8 valence electrons in Lab 0's two-atom silicon cell is one function of 24 coordinates: 102410^{24}1024 numbers, about 1.6×10251.6 \times 10^{25}1.6×1025 bytes as complex numbers, before spin is even counted. That is more than all the data storage on Earth. The density of the same electrons is a function of 3 coordinates: 10310^3103 real numbers, 8 kB.

Ten points per coordinate is far too coarse for real accuracy, which only widens the gap. And the same exponential governs the work of computing Ψ\PsiΨ, not just storing it, which is why wavefunction methods of chemical accuracy remain limited to systems with about ten chemically active electrons, unless they use clever approximations. Change N and the grid to see how fast the wall rises.

What do DFT codes store instead?

One-electron orbitals and the density: N functions of three coordinates, not one function of 3N.

  • Exact ΨOne function of 3N coordinates (plus spins). Grows exponentially with N.
  • Kohn–Sham orbitalsOne-electron orbitals (about N/2 occupied per k-point, two electrons each), each a function of 3 coordinates: WAVECAR.
  • DensityOne function of 3 coordinates: CHGCAR.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 511 / 15

Kohn–Sham DFT (Lecture 9) never needs the interacting Ψ\PsiΨ. It works with a fictitious system of non-interacting electrons whose wavefunction is a single Slater determinant of one-electron orbitals, constructed to have the same density as the real electrons. A determinant is fully specified by its N orbitals, so storing it costs N functions of three coordinates, a cost that grows only in proportion to N. The orbitals, one per band at each k-point, are what VASP keeps in WAVECAR, and the density is what it writes to CHGCAR.

The two effects of this lecture, exchange and correlation, do not disappear. In Kohn–Sham DFT their energies are collected, together with a kinetic-energy correction, into the exchange–correlation energy of Lecture 0, the one piece that must be approximated. That is why its name combines them.

Which misconceptions should you drop now?

Five beliefs about many electrons. Click a card to turn it over.

“The Pauli principle is a repulsive force.”

It is a symmetry requirement on Ψ. No force term in the Hamiltonian produces it.

“Antisymmetry is just bookkeeping.”

It changes where electrons can be: same-spin electrons never meet, and shells fill in order.

“A Slater determinant is the exact many-electron wavefunction.”

It includes exchange but misses correlation; some states need several determinants.

“Two electrons per orbital is a rule of thumb.”

It follows from antisymmetry: one per spin-orbital, two spins per spatial orbital.

“VASP stores the many-electron wavefunction.”

It stores one-electron orbitals (WAVECAR) and the density (CHGCAR).

What should you remember?

Electrons repel and are indistinguishable fermions: that is what makes many of them hard.

  • Without repulsion the problem separates; for helium that is 30 eV wrong, six bonds' worth.
  • Electrons have spin; a spin-orbital is a spatial orbital times α or β.
  • Electron wavefunctions are antisymmetric; a Slater determinant builds that in.
  • Antisymmetry gives Pauli exclusion and the exchange hole; it does not give correlation.
  • Ψ of a solid cannot be stored; codes store one-electron orbitals and the density.

Key terms

Spin-orbital
A one-electron function of position and spin, χ(x)=ϕ(r)σ(s)\chi(\mathbf x) = \phi(\mathbf r)\sigma(s)χ(x)=ϕ(r)σ(s): a spatial orbital times a spin function, α (up) or β (down).
Exchange hole
The region around an electron where another electron of the same spin is unlikely to be found, because the antisymmetric wavefunction vanishes when two same-spin electrons meet.
Slater determinant
An antisymmetric many-electron wavefunction built as the determinant of N spin-orbitals, with electrons in the rows and orbitals in the columns. Swapping two electrons swaps two rows and flips the sign.
Pauli exclusion principle
No two electrons can occupy the same spin-orbital, so a spatial orbital holds at most two electrons, of opposite spin. It follows from the antisymmetry of the wavefunction.
Electron correlation
The way electrons of either spin avoid each other because they repel, beyond what a single Slater determinant describes. The correlation energy is the difference between the exact and the Hartree–Fock energy.
