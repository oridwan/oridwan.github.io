Atomic orbitals, core and valence electrons

Where do s, p, d and f, and “core” versus “valence”, come from?

You will be able to explain

  • what n, ℓ and m label, and how they set an orbital's shape and nodes
  • why many-electron atoms fill their orbitals in the order they do, with exceptions such as Cr and Cu
  • what distinguishes core, semicore and valence electrons
  • what changes in a calculation when you choose Ti, Ti_pv or Ti_sv

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 41 / 14

Every POTCAR you choose makes a statement about atoms: which electrons take part in bonding, and which are frozen in a core. To read that statement you need the language of atomic orbitals: n, ℓ and m, s, p, d and f, shells, shielding and the filling order. This lecture builds that language from the one atom we can solve exactly, hydrogen, and ends with the choice between Ti, Ti_pv and Ti_sv.

Ti, Ti_pv or Ti_sv: 4, 10 or 12 valence electrons. The difference is which shells count as core.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 42 / 14

VASP provides three standard PBE datasets for titanium and recommends Ti_sv. They differ in which electrons are treated explicitly: "pv" adds the 3p shell to the valence, and "sv" adds the 3s shell as well. Each choice changes the number of electrons VASP handles and the cutoff it needs. To understand what is being chosen, and why it matters, we need to know what 3s, 3p, 3d and 4s are. That starts with hydrogen.

What labels the hydrogen atom's states?

What do s, p and d orbitals look like?

ℓ (with m) fixes the shape and the ℓ angular nodes; n − ℓ − 1 counts the radial nodes.

What changes when an atom has many electrons?

Inner electrons shield the nucleus, so orbitals with the same n no longer share one energy.

  • ShieldingElectrons in inner orbitals screen the nuclear charge seen by outer ones.
  • Penetrations orbitals reach the nucleus, so they feel more of its charge and drop lowest.
  • Centrifugal barrierHigh-ℓ orbitals are kept away from the nucleus and see less of it.

Result: within a shell, E(ns) < E(np) < E(nd). The hydrogen degeneracy is lifted.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 45 / 14

With more than one electron, the equation can no longer be solved exactly (Lecture 3), but the qualitative picture is clear. Think of one electron moving in the average field of the nucleus and all the other electrons. That field is still spherical, so the orbitals keep their s, p, d shapes, but the radial equation now feels an effective potential

Veff(r)=−Zeff(r)r+ℓ(ℓ+1)2r2.V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) = -\frac{Z_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)}{r} + \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2r^2}.Veff​(r)=−rZeff​(r)​+2r2ℓ(ℓ+1)​.

The first term is the nuclear attraction, screened by the electrons inside radius rrr: close to the nucleus the electron feels nearly the full charge ZZZ, far outside the other electrons only a net charge of about 1. The second term, the centrifugal barrier, comes from the angular motion and pushes electrons with larger ℓ\ellℓ away from the nucleus. Near the nucleus a radial function behaves as rℓr^\ellrℓ, so s orbitals have amplitude right at the nucleus, p orbitals start linearly, and d orbitals more slowly still.

So s electrons penetrate under the inner shells and feel more of the nuclear charge, and their energy drops furthest; p electrons less so, d electrons least. The degeneracy of hydrogen is lifted, Ens<Enp<EndE_{ns} < E_{np} < E_{nd}Ens​<Enp​<End​. Lecture 6 makes the screening quantitative for helium: each electron sees an effective charge of 27/16 ≈ 1.69 instead of 2. Computing this shielding from the electrons' mutual repulsion, in a mean field, is the job of the Hartree–Fock method (Lecture 6).

In what order do atoms fill their orbitals?

Mostly in the aufbau order, 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d, with exceptions such as Cr and Cu.

Titanium22[Ar] 3d² 4s²
Chromium24[Ar] 3d⁵ 4s (not 3d⁴ 4s²)
Copper29[Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s (not 3d⁹ 4s²)
  • Why 4s before 3dIn potassium, the 4s orbital penetrates the argon core more than 3d does.
  • A surpriseIn the transition metals, the electron removed first is 4s, not 3d.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 46 / 14

Combine the ordering Ens<Enp<EndE_{ns} < E_{np} < E_{nd}Ens​<Enp​<End​ with the Pauli exclusion principle, at most two electrons per orbital (Lecture 5), and fill the orbitals from the bottom: that is the aufbau, or building-up, rule. [Ne] and [Ar] abbreviate the filled shells of neon and argon.

Penetration explains the most famous oddity. In potassium, the 4s orbital has three inner lobes that reach through the argon core towards the nucleus. The nodeless 3d orbital lies almost entirely outside the core, where the nuclear charge is screened by all 18 core electrons. So 4s lies lower, potassium's last electron goes into 4s, and calcium is 4s². The configurations in the table are the measured ground states. Chromium and copper break the simple rule, taking only one 4s electron. The 3d and 4s energies are so close that the details of the repulsion between electrons decide the order, and here they favour a half-filled or filled d shell.

The order also does not survive ionization. Once the 3d shell starts to fill, the 3d electrons, sitting largely inside the 4s, shield the nucleus from the 4s electrons, and in scandium and the transition metals after it the electron removed first is 4s, not 3d. The lesson for calculations is that 3d and 4s lie close in energy, so their relative occupation responds to the chemical environment.

What are core and valence electrons?

Valence electrons make bonds; core electrons sit deep and barely change. Semicore lie in between.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 47 / 14

The valence electrons occupy the outermost, highest-energy orbitals and take part in bonding. The core electrons are tightly bound close to the nucleus, with binding energies of hundreds or thousands of eV, and hardly notice the neighbouring atoms. Between them, semicore states such as titanium's 3s and 3p lie deep enough to be core-like, tens of eV below the valence, but high enough to respond to short bonds, pressure or magnetism.

VASP's PAW datasets freeze the core: the core electrons keep the configuration they had in the atomic calculation that generated the dataset (the frozen-core approximation, Lecture 0). In the figure, Ti freezes 18 electrons, Ti_pv 12 and Ti_sv 10. The configuration listed for a dataset need not be the free atom's: Ti is listed as 3d³ 4s¹, while the atom's ground state is 3d² 4s². In the solid the valence electrons are redistributed self-consistently anyway; what the dataset fixes is how many there are and which shells are frozen.

When should semicore electrons be valence?

When bonding, magnetism or the method can disturb them.

  • BondingShort bonds or high pressure push neighbouring atoms into the semicore region.
  • MagnetismSpin polarization of the valence can polarize the semicore shells too.
  • Hybrid functionalsExact exchange couples valence and semicore states: use _pv or _sv whenever possible.
  • The price, titaniumNELECT up to 3 times larger (4 → 12 per atom) and ENMAX from 178 to 275 eV.
  • The ruleStart from the recommended dataset; test another if your property depends on it.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 48 / 14

A frozen core is exact only if the core really does not change. Three situations disturb semicore states:

  • Bonding. When atoms come close, under pressure or in short bonds, the neighbours' orbitals overlap the semicore shells, which then take part in the bonding.
  • Magnetism. A spin-polarized valence shell exerts a different exchange field on up and down semicore electrons, and frozen semicore shells cannot respond.
  • Hybrid functionals. Exact exchange (Lecture 6) couples every occupied orbital to every other, including the semicore ones, so freezing them is a worse approximation; VASP strongly recommends the _pv and _sv datasets for hybrids.

For the early 3d metals, VASP's recommended datasets are Ti_sv, V_sv and Cr_pv; the plain Ti, V and Cr datasets can give reasonable results but should be used with great care. In most cases, though, freezing the semicore makes little difference, and testing is the way to know.

Semicore electrons cost. More electrons means more bands to compute, and the deeper, more compact orbitals need more plane waves, which shows up as a larger ENMAX. In a compound the default cutoff is the largest ENMAX of all species, so the price depends on the partners: with oxygen (ENMAX 400 eV), the cutoff does not change between Ti and Ti_sv; only the number of electrons does.

Why are 3d and 4f electrons special?

They are the first d and f shells: no radial node, so they stay compact even as valence electrons.

  • No radial node1s, 2p, 3d, 4f are the first orbitals of their ℓ, and nodeless in r.
  • CompactIn hydrogen, ⟨r⟩ = 10.5 bohr for 3d against 13.5 for 3s; in titanium, the 3d shields the 4s from the nucleus.
  • ConsequenceCompounds with partly filled d and f shells include the strongly correlated materials that common functionals often describe poorly.
⟨r⟩nℓ=3n2−ℓ(ℓ+1)2 a0\langle r \rangle_{n\ell} = \frac{3n^2 - \ell(\ell+1)}{2}\ a_0⟨r⟩nℓ​=23n2−ℓ(ℓ+1)​ a0​
  • Mean distance from the nucleus for hydrogen; smaller for larger ℓ\ellℓ at the same nnn.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 49 / 14

Why does a radial node matter? Orbitals with the same ℓ\ellℓ must be orthogonal to each other. The 2s orbital must be orthogonal to 1s, which forces it to change sign once, a node, and pushes its main lobe outward; 3s must be orthogonal to both 1s and 2s, and so on. But 3d is the first d orbital: there is no lower d orbital to be orthogonal to, so it has no node and is free to stay compact. The same holds for 2p and 4f.

The formula on the slide, exact for hydrogen, puts numbers on it: at n=3n = 3n=3, ⟨r⟩\langle r\rangle⟨r⟩ is 13.5 bohr for 3s, 12.5 for 3p and 10.5 for 3d. In a transition metal the 3d shell lies largely inside the 4s, with which it shares the valence, and it shields the nucleus from the 4s, which is why 4s ionizes first.

This has two consequences you will meet in VASP. First, compact, nodeless orbitals have sharp features that need many plane waves: oxygen's nodeless 2p is why its ENMAX is 400 eV, more than twice titanium's. Second, compact d and f orbitals keep their electrons close together, so the repulsion between them is strong. Many compounds with partly filled d or f shells are "strongly correlated", and LDA and GGA functionals often describe them poorly; f elements are especially hard, because self-interaction errors are largest in strongly localized orbitals. A common fix is an on-site Hubbard U (Lecture 25).

NELECT is set from the POTCARs for a neutral cell: ZVAL times the number of atoms of each species. ENMAX is each dataset's suggested cutoff, and the default ENCUT is the largest ENMAX among the POTCARs.

LORBIT asks VASP to project every Kohn–Sham orbital onto spherical harmonics around each atom, giving its s, p, d and f contributions there. There is no unique way to divide an orbital among atoms, so the numbers are qualitative, but they are usually a good guide to the character of a band. That is how a band structure gets labelled "Ti 3d" or "O 2p" in Lecture 29.

Which misconceptions should you drop now?

Five beliefs about orbitals and POTCARs. Click a card to turn it over.

“All orbitals of a shell have the same energy.”

Only in hydrogen and other one-electron ions. In many-electron atoms shielding puts ns below np below nd.

“4s fills before 3d, so it must also empty last.”

4s fills first in K and Ca, but in transition metals 4s electrons are removed first.

“The aufbau rule always gives the ground state.”

Cr is 3d⁵ 4s and Cu 3d¹⁰ 4s: the simple rule fails for them.

“More valence electrons is always better.”

It costs electrons and cutoff. Use the recommended dataset and test when the property depends on it.

“The POTCAR configuration is the atom's ground state.”

Ti is listed as 3d³ 4s¹; the free atom is 3d² 4s². The dataset fixes the count, not the occupations.

How many radial and angular nodes does a 4d orbital have?

Angular: ℓ = 2. Radial: n − ℓ − 1 = 1. Three nodes in all, n − 1.

Why does potassium's last electron go into 4s, not 3d?

4s penetrates the argon core and feels more of the nuclear charge, so it lies lower than 3d there.

What makes titanium's 3s and 3p semicore?

They lie deep, below the 3d and 4s valence, but are disturbed enough by bonding or magnetism that VASP recommends treating them as valence.

What changes in a calculation when you switch from Ti to Ti_sv?

8 more valence electrons per Ti (ZVAL 4 → 12), more bands, and a higher ENMAX; the frozen core shrinks from 18 to 10 electrons.

What should you remember?

Atoms are hydrogen's orbitals, reordered by shielding and split into core and valence.

  • n, ℓ, m label hydrogen's states; ℓ gives s, p, d, f; there are n − ℓ − 1 radial and ℓ angular nodes.
  • Shielding and penetration put ns below np below nd; aufbau gives the filling order, with exceptions such as Cr and Cu.
  • Core electrons are frozen; valence electrons bond; semicore states sit between and can matter.
  • A POTCAR fixes ZVAL and ENMAX: Ti, Ti_pv, Ti_sv have 4, 10, 12 valence electrons.

Key terms

Valence electrons
The electrons in an atom's outermost, highest-energy orbitals, which take part in bonding. In VASP, the electrons a POTCAR treats explicitly; their number per atom is ZVAL.
Core electrons
Electrons in deep, tightly bound orbitals close to the nucleus, little affected by neighbouring atoms. A PAW dataset freezes them in the configuration used to generate it.
Semicore states
Orbitals that lie below the valence but high enough to respond to bonding, pressure or magnetism, such as titanium's 3s and 3p. VASP's _pv and _sv datasets treat them as valence.
Frozen-core approximation
The approximation that the core electrons keep the configuration of the isolated atom used to generate the PAW dataset.
