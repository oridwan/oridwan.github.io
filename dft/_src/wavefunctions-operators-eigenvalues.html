Lecture 1 ended with an equation, \(\hat H\psi = E\psi\), written for the electrons of one fixed geometry. This lecture slows down to say what each symbol means: the state \(\psi\), the operator \(\hat H\), the number \(E\). It uses the simplest system there is, one particle in a box, and ends with the numbers VASP writes for every band at every k-point.

What does VASP mean by a band?

At every k-point VASP solves an eigenvalue problem; its solutions are the bands.

\[ \eqterm{H}{\hat H_{\mathrm{KS}}}\; \eqterm{psi}{\psi_{n\mathbf k}} = \eqterm{eps}{\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}}\; \psi_{n\mathbf k} \]
  • An operator: the Kohn–Sham Hamiltonian, rebuilt at every SCF step ().
  • An eigenfunction: the orbital of band \(n\) at k-point \(\mathbf k\).
  • An eigenvalue: a number, its energy. EIGENVAL lists them all.
  • This lectureWhat an operator, an eigenfunction and an eigenvalue are, using one particle in a box.
  • LaterWhere k comes from () and why these eigenvalues are not quite measurable energies ().

After every run, VASP writes the file EIGENVAL, which lists the Kohn–Sham eigenvalues for every band at every k-point. Each of those numbers solves an equation of the form on the slide: an operator acts on a function and returns the same function multiplied by a number. Lecture 0 called this solving the Kohn–Sham equations. This lecture explains the vocabulary, with a system small enough to solve by hand.

What is a wavefunction?

A function ψ(x) whose squared magnitude, |ψ|², is a probability density.

The state of a quantum system is described by its wavefunction, \(\psi\). For one particle in three dimensions, \(|\psi(x, y, z)|^2\,dx\,dy\,dz\) is the probability of finding the particle in the small volume \(dx\,dy\,dz\) around the point \((x, y, z)\). So \(\psi\) itself is not a probability. It can be negative, and in general it is a complex number, \(\psi = u + iv\), whose squared magnitude \(|\psi|^2 = \psi^*\psi = u^2 + v^2\) is never negative. That squared magnitude is the probability density. In one dimension, the probability of finding the particle between \(a\) and \(b\) is

\[ P(a \le x \le b) = \int_a^b |\psi(x)|^2\, dx . \]

The particle must be somewhere, so the probability over all space is one: \(\int |\psi|^2\, dx = 1\). A wavefunction that satisfies this is normalized. Any solution can be normalized by dividing it by the square root of \(\int|\psi|^2dx\).

In the figure, the second state \(\psi_2\) is negative in the right half of the box, but \(|\psi_2|^2\) is not: the overall sign of \(\psi\) has no meaning on its own. Multiplying \(\psi\) by \(-1\), or by any \(e^{i\theta}\), changes no probability. Relative signs matter only when states are added together, as a later slide shows.

What is an operator?

A rule that turns one function into another. Every observable has one.

ObservableOperatorWhat it does to ψ(x)
Position\(\hat x\)Multiplies by \(x\): \(\hat x\,\psi = x\,\psi(x)\)
Momentum\(\hat p = -i\hbar\, \dfrac{d}{dx}\)Takes the slope, times \(-i\hbar\)
Kinetic energy\(\hat T = -\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{d^2}{dx^2}\)Takes the curvature, times \(-\hbar^2/2m\)
Total energy\(\hat H = \hat T + V(x)\)Kinetic plus potential energy: the Hamiltonian

An operator, written with a hat, takes a function and returns another function. Position simply multiplies by \(x\); the potential energy \(V(x)\), like position, just multiplies. Momentum differentiates. Why? A particle of momentum \(p\) has, by de Broglie's relation, a wave with wavelength \(h/p\), that is \(e^{ikx}\) with \(p = \hbar k\). The operator that pulls the number \(\hbar k\) out of \(e^{ikx}\) is \(-i\hbar\,d/dx\), since \(-i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dx}e^{ikx} = \hbar k\,e^{ikx}\). Kinetic energy is \(p^2/2m\), so its operator differentiates twice: \(\hat T = \hat p^2/2m = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\). In three dimensions \(d^2/dx^2\) becomes \(\nabla^2 = \partial^2/\partial x^2 + \partial^2/\partial y^2 + \partial^2/\partial z^2\).

Every observable quantity has an operator, and these operators share two properties. They are linear: \(\hat A(a\psi_1 + b\psi_2) = a\hat A\psi_1 + b\hat A\psi_2\), which is what will let us turn quantum mechanics into linear algebra at the end of this lecture. And they are Hermitian, which a later slide explains.

What is an eigenvalue equation?

An eigenfunction comes back from the operator unchanged, apart from a factor: the eigenvalue.

\[ \eqterm{A}{\hat A}\, \eqterm{f}{\psi} = \eqterm{a}{a}\, \psi \]
  • Operator: for example \(\hat p\) or \(\hat H\).
  • Eigenfunction (eigenstate) of that operator.
  • Eigenvalue: a number, the same everywhere in space.
  • An eigenfunction of momentum\(\hat p\, e^{ikx} = -i\hbar\,(ik)\, e^{ikx} = \hbar k\, e^{ikx}\)
  • Not an eigenfunction\(\hat p\, \sin kx = -i\hbar k \cos kx\): a different function comes back.

Most functions are changed by an operator into a different function. A few special ones come back as themselves, multiplied by a constant. They are the eigenfunctions of that operator, and the constants are its eigenvalues. "Eigen" is German for "own": the function's own value.

The plane wave \(e^{ikx}\) is an eigenfunction of momentum with eigenvalue \(\hbar k\): a state of definite momentum. The function \(\sin kx\) is not, because differentiating it gives a cosine. It is, however, a sum of two plane waves, \(\sin kx = (e^{ikx} - e^{-ikx})/2i\), with momenta \(+\hbar k\) and \(-\hbar k\); a later slide shows what measuring the momentum of such a sum gives. Notice that \(\sin kx\) is an eigenfunction of the kinetic-energy operator, with eigenvalue \(\hbar^2k^2/2m\): both of its plane waves have the same kinetic energy. Keep the plane wave in mind; in VASP's whole basis is made of them.

What does Ĥψ = Eψ ask for?

The states with a definite energy: Ĥ returns them multiplied by a number, E.

\[ \eqterm{T}{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi_n}{dx^2}} + \eqterm{V}{V(x)\,\psi_n} = \eqterm{E}{E_n}\, \psi_n \]
  • Kinetic energy: large where \(\psi\) curves sharply.
  • Potential energy, point by point.
  • Energy eigenvalue of state \(n\).
  • Many solutions\(\psi_1, \psi_2, \psi_3, \dots\) with energies \(E_1 \le E_2 \le E_3 \le \dots\)
  • Ground stateThe lowest one. Higher ones are excited states.

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is an eigenvalue equation for the energy operator. It does not ask for the wavefunction; it asks for the special states whose energy is definite, and for their energies. They are called stationary states for a reason. Under the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, an energy eigenstate only acquires a rotating phase, \(\psi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\), so its probability density \(|\psi_n|^2\) never changes. An electron in a stationary state of an atom does not move around in any measurable way.

There are usually many solutions, ordered by energy. The lowest is the ground state, the state DFT is built to describe; the others are excited states. The equation also shows where kinetic energy comes from: the second derivative is large where \(\psi\) bends sharply, so a wavefunction squeezed into a small region, or one with many wiggles, has high kinetic energy. On the next slide, boundary conditions decide which solutions exist.

Why does energy come in steps?

Only standing waves that vanish at both walls are allowed, so only certain energies are.

Put a particle between two infinitely high walls a distance \(L\) apart. Inside, \(V = 0\), and the Schrödinger equation reads \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' = E\psi\). Its general solution is \(\psi = A\sin kx + B\cos kx\) with \(E = \hbar^2k^2/2m\). The particle cannot be inside an infinite wall, so \(\psi\) must vanish at both walls:

  • \(\psi(0) = 0\) removes the cosine: \(B = 0\).
  • \(\psi(L) = A\sin kL = 0\) requires \(kL = n\pi\): a whole number of half-wavelengths must fit in the box.
  • Normalizing, \(\int_0^L A^2\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,dx = A^2L/2 = 1\), gives \(A = \sqrt{2/L}\).

So

\[ \psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{L}}\, \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad E_n = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Big(\frac{n\pi}{L}\Big)^2 = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8 m L^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]

The energy is quantized not because anything forbids other energies in principle, but because only these waves fit. State \(n\) has \(n - 1\) nodes and energy \(n^2 E_1\). Two lessons carry over to real materials. More nodes mean more curvature, so more kinetic energy. And confinement costs energy: halve the box and every level rises four times. For an electron in a box 1 Å long, about the size of an atom, \(E_1 = h^2/8m_eL^2 = 37.6\) eV, the same order as the energies that bind electrons in atoms. Use the slider to see how fast the levels rise as the box shrinks.

What if the state is not an eigenstate?

Each measurement still gives one eigenvalue; the coefficients decide how often.

Because the Schrödinger equation is linear, any sum of solutions is also a valid state. Any state can be written as a superposition of eigenstates, \(\psi = \sum_n c_n \psi_n\), and such a state has no definite energy. Yet a measurement of the energy always gives one of the eigenvalues, never anything in between. Which one is a matter of chance: eigenvalue \(E_n\) turns up with probability \(P_n = |c_n|^2\). For a normalized \(\psi\) these probabilities add up to one, \(\sum_n |c_n|^2 = 1\).

Try it: set \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) and press Measure once a few times. Every result is \(E_1\) or \(4E_1\), never the average. Press Measure 100 times and the bars approach \(|c_1|^2\) and \(|c_2|^2\).

Notice also that the probability density of the superposition is not just the sum of the two densities. For real coefficients,

\[ |c_1\psi_1 + c_2\psi_2|^2 = c_1^2\psi_1^2 + c_2^2\psi_2^2 + 2c_1c_2\,\psi_1\psi_2 . \]

The cross term is interference. In the box, \(\psi_1\psi_2\) is positive in the left half and negative in the right half, so with \(c_1c_2 > 0\) the particle is more likely on the left, and flipping the sign of \(c_2\) moves it to the right. That is why the relative sign, or phase, of the parts of \(\psi\) matters.

Is an expectation value a possible result?

Not necessarily. In an eigenstate every measurement gives it; otherwise it is an average.

\[ \langle A \rangle = \eqterm{int}{\int \psi^*\, \hat A\, \psi \, dx} = \eqterm{sum}{\sum_n |c_n|^2\, a_n} \]
  • Expectation value of \(\hat A\) in a normalized state \(\psi\).
  • The same number: each eigenvalue weighted by its probability.
  • Eigenstate ψ₂Every measurement gives \(4E_1\), and \(\langle E\rangle = 4E_1\).
  • Equal superposition of ψ₁ and ψ₂Measurements give \(E_1\) or \(4E_1\); \(\langle E\rangle = 2.5\,E_1\), which never occurs.

The average of many measurements on identically prepared states is the expectation value. The two forms on the slide are the same number. Insert \(\psi = \sum_n c_n\psi_n\) into the integral and use \(\hat A\psi_n = a_n\psi_n\):

\[ \int \psi^*\hat A\psi\,dx = \sum_{m,n} c_m^* c_n\, a_n \int \psi_m^*\psi_n\,dx = \sum_n |c_n|^2 a_n , \]

because the eigenstates are orthonormal (\(\int\psi_m^*\psi_n\,dx\) is 1 for \(m = n\) and 0 otherwise; a later slide explains why). The integral form is the one you compute; the sum form says what it means.

If \(\psi\) is an eigenstate of \(\hat A\), only one term survives and every measurement gives the eigenvalue. Otherwise the average can fall between the possible results. For the equal superposition, \(\langle E\rangle = \tfrac12 E_1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 4E_1 = 2.5\,E_1\), a value no single measurement ever gives. This distinction returns in : the energy of a trial wavefunction is an expectation value, \(\langle \psi|\hat H|\psi\rangle\), and the sum form shows at once that it can never fall below the lowest eigenvalue, the ground-state energy.

Why must observables be Hermitian?

Hermitian operators have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenstates that form a complete set.

  • Real eigenvaluesEvery measured value is a real number.
  • Orthogonal eigenstates\(\int \psi_m^*\, \psi_n\, dx = \delta_{mn}\) once normalized: 1 if \(m = n\), otherwise 0.
  • Expansion\(\psi = \sum_n c_n \psi_n\) with \(c_n = \int \psi_n^*\, \psi\, dx\).

An operator is Hermitian if it can be moved from one function to the other inside an integral, with a complex conjugate:

\[ \int f^*\,(\hat A g)\,dx = \int (\hat A f)^*\, g\,dx \quad \text{for all } f, g. \]

Multiplication by a real function, such as \(x\) or \(V(x)\), obviously has this property. For \(\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx\) it follows from integrating by parts, with the boundary terms vanishing because the wavefunctions go to zero far away; the factor \(-i\) is exactly what makes it work. Three consequences make the whole machinery of quantum mechanics work:

  • Real eigenvalues. Every possible measurement result is a real number.
  • Orthogonal eigenstates. Eigenstates with different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and degenerate ones can always be chosen orthogonal. The box states are an example: \(\int_0^L \psi_1\psi_2\,dx = 0\).
  • Completeness. The eigenstates form a complete set: any reasonable state can be expanded as \(\psi = \sum_n c_n\psi_n\). Orthonormality then makes each coefficient a simple overlap integral. Multiply the expansion by \(\psi_m^*\) and integrate: every term but one vanishes, leaving \(c_m = \int\psi_m^*\psi\,dx\).
Proof of the first two properties

Let \(\hat A\psi_n = a_n\psi_n\). Take \(f = g = \psi_n\) in the definition: the left side is \(a_n\int|\psi_n|^2dx\) and the right side is \(a_n^*\int|\psi_n|^2dx\). So \(a_n = a_n^*\): the eigenvalue is real.

Now take \(f = \psi_m\) and \(g = \psi_n\). The left side is \(a_n\int\psi_m^*\psi_n\,dx\) and the right side, using that \(a_m\) is real, is \(a_m\int\psi_m^*\psi_n\,dx\). Subtracting, \((a_n - a_m)\int\psi_m^*\psi_n\,dx = 0\). If \(a_n \ne a_m\), the integral must vanish: the two eigenstates are orthogonal.

What does ⟨ψ|Ĥ|ψ⟩ mean?

Bra–ket notation is shorthand for the integrals on the previous slides.

Bra–ketIntegralMeaning
\(\langle \psi \vert \psi \rangle = 1\)\(\int \psi^*\psi\, dx = 1\)Normalization
\(\langle \psi_m \vert \psi_n \rangle = \delta_{mn}\)\(\int \psi_m^*\psi_n\, dx\)Orthonormality
\(c_n = \langle \psi_n \vert \psi \rangle\)\(\int \psi_n^*\psi\, dx\)How much of \(\psi_n\) is in \(\psi\)
\(\langle \psi \vert \hat H \vert \psi \rangle\)\(\int \psi^* \hat H \psi\, dx\)Energy expectation value
\(\langle \phi_i \vert \hat H \vert \phi_j \rangle\)\(\int \phi_i^* \hat H \phi_j\, dx\)A matrix element, \(H_{ij}\)

Electronic-structure papers and the VASP documentation write states as kets, \(|\psi\rangle\), and their complex conjugates as bras, \(\langle\psi|\). A bra next to a ket means: multiply the conjugate of the first function by the second and integrate over all coordinates (and sum over spin, if there is spin). An operator in the middle acts on the ket first. Nothing new is introduced; the notation only saves writing, and it makes the analogy with vectors visible: \(\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle\) behaves like a dot product, and orthonormal states behave like perpendicular unit vectors. The last row is the bridge to the next slide.

How does a computer solve Ĥψ = Eψ?

In a finite basis, ψ becomes a list of coefficients and Ĥ a matrix: Hc = Ec.

\[ \eqterm{exp}{\psi = \sum_{j=1}^{M} c_j\, \phi_j} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \eqterm{Hm}{\begin{pmatrix} H_{11} & H_{12} & \cdots \\ H_{21} & H_{22} & \cdots \\ \vdots & & \ddots \end{pmatrix}} \eqterm{c}{\begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix}} = \eqterm{E}{E} \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix} \]
  • Basis expansion: \(M\) known functions \(\phi_j\), here orthonormal.
  • Hamiltonian matrix, \(H_{ij} = \langle \phi_i | \hat H | \phi_j \rangle\): numbers a computer can store.
  • Eigenvector: the coefficients of one state.
  • Eigenvalue: its energy. An \(M \times M\) matrix has \(M\) of them.

A computer cannot store a function at infinitely many points. It can store numbers. So expand the unknown state in a set of known basis functions, \(\psi = \sum_j c_j\phi_j\), and let the coefficients carry the information. Insert the expansion into \(\hat H\psi = E\psi\) and take the overlap with one basis function, \(\phi_i\):

\[ \sum_j \langle\phi_i|\hat H|\phi_j\rangle\, c_j = E \sum_j \langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle\, c_j = E\, c_i . \]

The last step uses orthonormality. Doing this for every \(i\) gives \(M\) equations, which together are the matrix equation on the slide, \(\mathbf H\mathbf c = E\mathbf c\). The differential equation has become a problem of linear algebra. Because \(\hat H\) is Hermitian, the matrix is Hermitian too (\(H_{ji} = H_{ij}^*\)), and standard library routines diagonalize it, returning all \(M\) eigenvalues and eigenvectors at once.

With a complete, infinite basis this would be exact. In practice the basis is finite, so the answer is approximate. How good it is, and what happens when the basis functions are not orthogonal (the right side becomes \(E\sum_j S_{ij}c_j\), with the overlap matrix \(S_{ij} = \langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle\), so \(\mathbf H\mathbf c = E\,\mathbf S\mathbf c\)), is the subject of . VASP's basis is plane waves, and ENCUT sets how many (). Even so, VASP's PAW method introduces an overlap operator \(\hat S\), so what it actually solves has the form \((\hat H - \varepsilon \hat S)\psi = 0\); explains why.

Where do these objects live in VASP?

Orbitals in WAVECAR, eigenvalues in EIGENVAL and OUTCAR, their number set by NBANDS.

In this lectureIn VASPWhere to find it
Operator \(\hat H\)Kohn–Sham Hamiltonian \(\hat H_{\mathrm{KS}}\), rebuilt every SCF step,
Eigenfunction \(\psi_n\)Orbital \(\psi_{n\mathbf k}\): band \(n\), k-point \(\mathbf k\)WAVECAR
Eigenvalue \(E_n\)Band energy \(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}\), in eVEIGENVAL, OUTCAR
Number of states solved forNBANDS: occupied bands plus some empty onesINCAR, OUTCAR
Probability density \(|\psi|^2\)Density \(n(\mathbf r) = \sum_{n\mathbf k} w_{\mathbf k} f_{n\mathbf k} |\psi_{n\mathbf k}|^2\), with k-point weights \(w_{\mathbf k}\)CHGCAR

NBANDS sets how many Kohn–Sham orbitals VASP computes at each k-point. It needs at least all the occupied ones plus one empty band, and its default adds a margin, which is a safe choice in most cases. Empty bands are needed because the iterative solver converges better with them, and because properties such as the band gap or optical spectra need the lowest unoccupied states. The number of electrons, NELECT, is taken from the POTCARs for a neutral cell: the valence electrons per atom, ZVAL, times the number of atoms of each species.

One orbital is not the electron density. The density adds up the occupied orbitals, each weighted by its occupation \(f_{n\mathbf k}\) (up to two electrons per orbital without spin polarization) and by the weight \(w_{\mathbf k}\) of its k-point; the weights add up to one. In OUTCAR, every k-point gets a block that starts with band No. and lists band energies and occupations.

Are VASP's eigenvalues measurable energies?

Not in general: they belong to an auxiliary problem, not to the real interacting electrons.

\[ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{HOMO}} = -I \]
  • The one exact link: with the exact functional, the highest occupied eigenvalue of a finite system is minus its first ionization energy \(I\). The other eigenvalues have no exact meaning.
  • What they areEigenvalues of the Kohn–Sham Hamiltonian, a stand-in system of non-interacting electrons ().
  • Still usefulOften a fair, qualitative guide to single-particle energies.
  • Watch outThe band gap they give is not the measured gap ().

Everything in this lecture so far was exact quantum mechanics for one particle: each eigenvalue is a possible result of an energy measurement. The Kohn–Sham equations that VASP solves look the same, but their Hamiltonian describes a fictitious system of non-interacting electrons, constructed to have the same density as the real, interacting ones. Its eigenvalues are energies of that fictitious system.

Even with the exact exchange–correlation functional, only one of them has an exact physical meaning: the highest occupied eigenvalue of a finite system, an atom or molecule, equals minus its first ionization energy. In a periodic cell even this link cannot be used directly, because the eigenvalues have no vacuum level to be measured from. The others have no exact meaning, and in particular the Kohn–Sham gap differs from the true gap even with the exact functional, by a term called the derivative discontinuity ().

In practice, Kohn–Sham eigenvalues often approximate the energies of adding or removing an electron well enough to be qualitatively useful: band dispersions, orderings and densities of states usually look right, while band gaps come out too small. So read band structures with care until and .

Which misconceptions should you drop now?

Five confusions about wavefunctions and eigenvalues. Click a card to turn it over.

“ψ is the probability of finding the particle.”

|ψ|² is a probability density. ψ itself can be negative or complex.

“The expectation value is the most likely result.”

It is an average. In a superposition it may lie between eigenvalues and never be measured.

“The sign of ψ means nothing.”

Alone it does not, but relative signs between added states change |ψ|² through interference.

“One orbital is the electron density.”

The density adds up all occupied orbitals, each weighted by its occupation.

“Every VASP eigenvalue is a measured energy.”

Kohn–Sham eigenvalues belong to an auxiliary system and need careful interpretation.

Check yourself

One question per outcome. Answer aloud, then click to compare.

What is the difference between ψ and |ψ|²?

ψ is the amplitude, possibly negative or complex. |ψ|² is the probability density; integrated over a region, it gives a probability.

In \(\hat A\psi = a\psi\), name each symbol.

\(\hat A\) is an operator, ψ an eigenfunction of it, and a the eigenvalue: a number.

ψ = (ψ₁ + ψ₂)/√2 in a box. What can an energy measurement give, and what is ⟨E⟩?

E₁ or 4E₁, each with probability ½. ⟨E⟩ = 2.5 E₁, which never occurs.

How does a basis turn Ĥψ = Eψ into something a computer can solve?

ψ = Σ c_j φ_j turns it into Hc = Ec in an orthonormal basis (Hc = ESc otherwise), with H_ij = ⟨φ_i|Ĥ|φ_j⟩.

What does NBANDS set, and are its eigenvalues measured energies?

The number of Kohn–Sham orbitals per k-point. Their eigenvalues belong to an auxiliary problem, so not in general.

What should you remember?

State → wavefunction; observable → operator; possible result → eigenvalue.

  • |ψ|² is a probability density, and a normalized ψ integrates to one.
  • An eigenfunction comes back from its operator times a number; Ĥψ = Eψ asks for the states of definite energy.
  • A measurement gives an eigenvalue, with probability |c_n|²; the expectation value is the average and may never be measured.
  • In an orthonormal basis, Ĥψ = Eψ becomes Hc = Ec (in general Hc = ESc). VASP solves such a problem for NBANDS orbitals at every k-point.
  • Kohn–Sham eigenvalues are not, in general, measurable excitation energies.
