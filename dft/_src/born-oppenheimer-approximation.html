Lecture 0 treated VASP as a machine that turns a structure into an energy. This lecture opens the machine one level. Which physical problem does it solve? Which approximations are already made before you choose any functional? And what actually happens, step by step, when a structure relaxes?

What is VASP computing when a structure relaxes?

Push an atom off its site and the forces push it back. Where do those forces come from?

This lecture follows the question from the bottom up:

  1. Particles
  2. Hamiltonian
  3. Born–Oppenheimer
  4. Energy surface
  5. Forces
  6. Relaxation

In Lab 1 you will take the silicon crystal of Lab 0, push one atom of the cell slightly off its site, and let VASP relax it. In a periodic calculation every copy of that atom moves with it, so in effect one set of atoms shifts against the other, as in the sketch. The forces then push the two sets back toward their ideal positions.

VASP does not know that a crystal “wants” to be perfect. It computes energies and forces from a quantum-mechanical model, and a relaxation happens because it keeps asking one question: if the nuclei move a little, does the energy go down? This lecture builds that question from the bottom up, starting with the particles a material is made of.

What is a material made of, as far as VASP is concerned?

Nuclei and electrons, held together by electrostatic attraction and repulsion.

For everything in this course, a material is a collection of nuclei and electrons interacting through electrostatic (Coulomb) forces: like charges repel, opposite charges attract. Nucleus \(I\) has charge \(+Z_I e\), mass \(M_I\) and position \(\mathbf R_I\); electron \(i\) has charge \(-e\), mass \(m_e\) and position \(\mathbf r_i\). Nuclei are about \(10^{-15}\) m across, a hundred thousand times smaller than an atom, so treating them as point charges is an excellent approximation.

The mass ratio is the key number of this lecture. Even a single proton is 1836.15 times heavier than an electron, and a silicon nucleus is about 51,200 times heavier. Electrons are light and behave as waves, so they need quantum mechanics: their state is a wavefunction of all their coordinates, \(\Psi(\mathbf r_1, \dots, \mathbf r_N)\), which for 100 electrons is a function of 300 spatial coordinates plus spins. For the heavy nuclei, a classical picture is often a good first approximation in ordinary DFT work; the end of this lecture says when it is not.

What does the full Hamiltonian contain?

Five terms: two kinetic energies and three Coulomb interactions.

\[ \hat H = \eqterm{TN}{\hat T_N} + \eqterm{Te}{\hat T_e} + \eqterm{VNN}{\hat V_{NN}} + \eqterm{Vee}{\hat V_{ee}} + \eqterm{VeN}{\hat V_{eN}} \]
  • Nuclear kinetic energy, \(-\sum_I \frac{1}{2M_I}\nabla_I^2\): the nuclei can move.
  • Electronic kinetic energy, \(-\sum_i \frac12\nabla_i^2\): the electrons can move.
  • Nucleus–nucleus repulsion, \(\sum_{I<J} \frac{Z_I Z_J}{|\mathbf R_I-\mathbf R_J|}\).
  • Electron–electron repulsion, \(\sum_{i<j} \frac{1}{|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j|}\): makes the problem many-body.
  • Electron–nucleus attraction, \(-\sum_{i,I} \frac{Z_I}{|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf R_I|}\): ties electrons to nuclei.

Hartree atomic units, with \(M_I\) in electron masses. Click a term, a line of the legend, or a group in the diagram.

The Hamiltonian is the operator for the total energy. In the non-relativistic picture it has five pieces: the kinetic energies of nuclei and electrons, and the three kinds of Coulomb interaction between them. The formulas are in Hartree atomic units, where \(\hbar = m_e = e = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1\). In SI units the electron–electron term, for example, reads \(e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 |\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_j|)\); atomic units simply set the constants to 1, which is why the formulas look bare. \(\nabla_I^2\) differentiates with respect to the coordinates of nucleus \(I\), and \(\nabla_i^2\) with respect to those of electron \(i\).

Two terms deserve attention. \(\hat V_{ee}\) depends on the distance between every pair of electrons, so the equation cannot be split into separate equations for each electron; that is what "many-body" means (). \(\hat V_{eN}\) depends on electron and nuclear coordinates together, so the Hamiltonian cannot be split into a nuclear part and an electronic part either. The next slides show how the second coupling is handled.

A note on relativity. The Hamiltonian on the slide is non-relativistic, but VASP's PAW datasets are not: they are built from scalar-relativistic calculations of the atom, which include the relativistic effects that matter most for core and heavy-atom electrons. What VASP leaves out by default is spin–orbit coupling, which LSORBIT = .TRUE. adds as a further term ().

Why can't we solve this Hamiltonian directly?

Every particle is coupled to every other, so the problem does not split into pieces.

90 coordinates

in one function, for the two-atom cell of silicon: 3 × (2 nuclei + 28 electrons).

Keep in mind

This is not a weakness of VASP. It is the central difficulty of quantum many-body physics, and every method in this course is a way around it.

The exact solution of the full problem is a single function of the \(3N\) electronic and \(3M\) nuclear coordinates, plus spins, and the Coulomb terms couple all of them. Even the two-atom primitive cell of silicon holds 2 nuclei and 28 electrons (silicon has \(Z = 14\)), which makes \(3 \times 30 = 90\) coordinates. A real crystal repeats that cell an enormous number of times. ( and show how periodicity helps.)

The first step around the difficulty is to separate the nuclei from the electrons. That still leaves \(\Psi(\mathbf r_1, \dots, \mathbf r_N)\) and the exponential wall of Lecture 0, which DFT then sidesteps.

What is the Born–Oppenheimer approximation?

The nuclei are so heavy that, to the electrons, they are almost standing still.

\[ E_{\mathrm{el}} \;:\; E_{\mathrm{vib}} \;:\; E_{\mathrm{rot}} \;\approx\; 1 \;:\; \Big(\frac{m_e}{M}\Big)^{1/2} \;:\; \frac{m_e}{M} \]
  • Electronic excitations, vibrations and rotations come in separate energy ranges, each smaller than the last by about \((m_e/M)^{1/2}\).
  • Electrons: light, fastRespond within about a femtosecond. Solved quantum mechanically, for nuclei held fixed.
  • Nuclei: at least 1836 times heavier, slowSilicon's fastest vibration takes 64 fs. Clamped while the electrons are solved, moved afterwards.

The nuclei are so much heavier than the electrons that they move much more slowly. A common picture is gnats flying around on the back of an elephant: the elephant may be walking, but from the gnats' point of view it is almost standing still. So we fix the nuclear positions, solve for the electrons alone, and only then let the nuclei move.

Numbers make this concrete.

  • Speeds. At the same kinetic energy, speed scales as one over the square root of the mass. A silicon nucleus, about 51,200 electron masses, moves some 226 times more slowly than an electron.
  • Times. An electronic energy scale of 1 eV corresponds to a time \(\hbar/(1\ \mathrm{eV}) = 0.66\) fs. The fastest vibration of silicon, the 520 cm⁻¹ optical phonon seen in Raman spectra, has a period of 64 fs.
  • Energies. Born and Oppenheimer showed that the energies separate as on the slide. In the H₂ molecule, exciting the electrons takes about 11 eV, one quantum of vibration 0.52 eV, and the rotational energy scale is 7.5 meV, each smaller than the last by a factor of 20 to 70, in line with \((m_e/M)^{1/2} \approx 1/43\) for a proton.

This is the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, published by Max Born and J. Robert Oppenheimer in 1927, and also called the clamped-nucleus approximation. It is the reason a VASP run can treat the positions in POSCAR as fixed numbers.

What problem is left once the nuclei are clamped?

Each geometry becomes its own electronic problem, with its own energy E(R).

\[ \eqterm{H}{\hat H_{\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf R)}\;\eqterm{psi}{\psi_{\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf r;\mathbf R)} = \eqterm{E}{E_{\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf R)}\;\psi_{\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf r;\mathbf R), \qquad \hat H_{\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf R) = \hat T_e + \hat V_{eN} + \hat V_{ee} + \eqterm{VNN}{V_{NN}(\mathbf R)} \]
  • Clamped-nuclei Hamiltonian: the full Hamiltonian without \(\hat T_N\), for nuclei fixed at \(\mathbf R\).
  • Electronic wavefunction: a function of the electron coordinates \(\mathbf r\); \(\mathbf R\) enters only as a parameter.
  • Electronic energy for this one geometry. Change \(\mathbf R\) and you have a new problem.
  • Nucleus–nucleus repulsion: for fixed nuclei a constant, which shifts the energy and leaves \(\psi\) unchanged.

The sentence that matters for VASP

A geometry goes in, an energy comes out: \(\{\mathbf R_I\} \longrightarrow E(\{\mathbf R_I\})\).

With the nuclei clamped, their kinetic energy drops out and their positions become numbers instead of variables. What remains is the electronic Schrödinger equation on the slide, one for every geometry. The semicolon in \(\psi_{\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf r;\mathbf R)\) is a reminder of this difference: \(\mathbf r\) are the variables, \(\mathbf R\) the parameters. The nucleus–nucleus repulsion is just a constant for fixed nuclei, so it shifts the energy without changing the wavefunction. VASP's total energy includes it.

Solving at many geometries traces out the potential-energy curve, or surface, on which the nuclei then move. That surface is the subject of the next slide.

What exactly is neglected, and when does it matter?

Write the exact wavefunction of electrons and nuclei as an expansion in the clamped-nuclei states, \(\Psi(\mathbf r, \mathbf R) = \sum_k \chi_k(\mathbf R)\, \psi_k(\mathbf r; \mathbf R)\). Here \(\psi_k\) are the electronic states at geometry \(\mathbf R\), with energies \(E_k(\mathbf R)\), and \(\chi_k\) are nuclear functions to be found. Insert this into the full Schrödinger equation, multiply by \(\psi_k^*\) and integrate over the electron coordinates. The result is

\[ \big[\hat T_N + E_k(\mathbf R)\big]\chi_k + \sum_l \Lambda_{kl}\,\chi_l = E\,\chi_k , \qquad \Lambda_{kl} = -\sum_I \frac{1}{M_I}\Big(\langle\psi_k|\nabla_I\psi_l\rangle\cdot\nabla_I + \tfrac12\langle\psi_k|\nabla_I^2\psi_l\rangle\Big). \]

The terms \(\Lambda_{kl}\) appear because the nuclear kinetic energy also acts on the electronic states, which depend on \(\mathbf R\). They carry a factor \(1/M_I\). The off-diagonal ones, \(k \ne l\), are the nonadiabatic couplings: they let the motion of the nuclei kick the electrons from one state into another. Dropping all of \(\Lambda\) leaves \(\big[\hat T_N + E_k(\mathbf R)\big]\chi_k = E\chi_k\): the nuclei move on the potential-energy surface \(E_k(\mathbf R)\) of a single electronic state. That is the Born–Oppenheimer approximation. The diagonal term \(\Lambda_{kk}\), the Born–Oppenheimer diagonal correction, is of relative size about \(m_e/M \sim 10^{-4}\); it is dropped too, except for very high accuracy, as in spectroscopy.

When does the approximation fail? For \(k \ne l\) the coupling can be rewritten as

\[ \langle\psi_k|\nabla_I\psi_l\rangle = \frac{\langle\psi_k|(\nabla_I \hat H_{\mathrm{el}})|\psi_l\rangle}{E_l - E_k}. \]

The energy difference in the denominator means the coupling becomes large when two electronic states come close in energy, as near an avoided crossing of two potential-energy curves. That is the territory of photochemistry and of some reactions.

Metals have no gap at all, yet in practice the approximation works well there for reaction energies, molecular dynamics and most phonon frequencies. Its failures in metals are subtler: in graphene whose electron density is tuned by a gate voltage, for example, it misses the measured stiffening of one Raman-active vibration, the G peak.

What is a potential-energy surface?

Solving at many geometries maps out the energy surface on which the nuclei move.

Solve the clamped-nuclei problem at many geometries and you trace out the potential-energy surface \(E(\mathbf R_1, \dots, \mathbf R_M)\). Its minima are stable or metastable structures. Its first-order saddle points, maxima along one direction and minima along all others, are the transition states, whose energies set the barriers between minima ().

A real material has \(3M\) nuclear coordinates, so its surface lives in many dimensions; the curve in the figure is a one-dimensional slice through a model bond. The Morse form is a simple formula that behaves like a real bond: \(D_e\) is the depth of the well, the energy needed to break the bond; \(r_e\) is the equilibrium bond length; and \(a\) sets how narrow the well is. The parameters are chosen for illustration, not taken from a real molecule. Pull the atoms together and the energy rises steeply; pull them apart and it levels off at zero, the energy of separated atoms. Press Relax to watch the atoms slide downhill; each row of the table is one ionic step, and the slides after next explain what that step does.

How does an energy surface give forces on atoms?

The force on a nucleus is the downhill slope of the energy.

\[ \eqterm{F}{\mathbf F_I} = \eqterm{minus}{-}\,\eqterm{grad}{\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf R_I}} \]
  • Force on nucleus \(I\), in eV/Å in VASP.
  • Slope of the energy as nucleus \(I\) moves, in each of three directions.
  • Minus sign: the force points downhill.
  • Stationary pointEvery force is zero: a minimum, a maximum or a saddle point.
  • MinimumThe energy also rises in every direction, apart from moving all atoms together.
  • In OUTCARThe block headed POSITION … TOTAL-FORCE (eV/Angst).

The force on a nucleus is the negative slope of the energy: it points in the direction that lowers the energy fastest, and its size says how fast. VASP prints the forces in eV/Å, in the OUTCAR block headed POSITION TOTAL-FORCE (eV/Angst), one line per atom with its position and the three force components.

Where the slope vanishes, every force is zero: at a minimum, a maximum or a saddle point. At a minimum the energy also rises in every direction, with one exception in a crystal: moving all atoms together costs nothing. In the bond of the previous figure, the equilibrium distance is where \(dE/dR = 0\). Pushed closer, the atoms are pushed apart; pulled further, they are pulled back.

How VASP gets forces without moving any atom

Moving each atom a little and recomputing the energy would take extra calculations for every coordinate. Instead, VASP uses the Hellmann–Feynman theorem. Differentiate \(E = \langle\psi|\hat H_{\mathrm{el}}|\psi\rangle\) with respect to \(\mathbf R_I\):

\[ \frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf R_I} = \Big\langle \psi \Big| \frac{\partial \hat H_{\mathrm{el}}}{\partial \mathbf R_I} \Big| \psi \Big\rangle + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\Big\langle \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \mathbf R_I} \Big| \hat H_{\mathrm{el}} \Big| \psi \Big\rangle . \]

Because \(\psi\) is an eigenstate, \(\hat H_{\mathrm{el}}\psi = E\psi\), the second term equals \(E\,\partial\langle\psi|\psi\rangle/\partial\mathbf R_I\), which is zero because \(\psi\) stays normalized. Only the first term remains, and only \(\hat V_{eN}\) and \(V_{NN}\) depend on \(\mathbf R_I\). The result has a classical reading: the force on a nucleus is its charge times the electric field at its position, created by the electron density \(n(\mathbf r)\) and by the other nuclei,

\[ \mathbf F_I = Z_I\Big[\sum_{J\ne I} Z_J\,\frac{\mathbf R_I - \mathbf R_J}{|\mathbf R_I - \mathbf R_J|^3} - \int n(\mathbf r)\,\frac{\mathbf R_I - \mathbf r}{|\mathbf R_I - \mathbf r|^3}\,d\mathbf r\Big]. \]

The first term pushes nucleus \(I\) away from the other nuclei; the second pulls it towards the electron cloud. So once the density is known, the forces cost almost nothing extra. In practice the PAW method adds terms from the atom-centred functions that move with each atom, and an SCF loop that is not fully converged adds a small error; covers both.

What is a structural relaxation?

A loop: solve the electrons, compute the forces, move the nuclei downhill, repeat.

A structural relaxation moves the nuclei downhill on the potential-energy surface, solving the whole electronic problem again at every step:

  1. Start from the positions in POSCAR and hold the nuclei fixed.
  2. Solve the electronic problem: the SCF loop of Lecture 0.
  3. Compute the energy and the forces.
  4. If every force is below the threshold, stop. Otherwise move the nuclei downhill, in a direction the optimizer builds from the forces, and go back to step 2.

Every ionic step contains a full electronic calculation, so a relaxation that takes 30 ionic steps solves the electronic problem 30 times. That is why relaxations cost far more than single-point runs. (Each new SCF starts from the previous step's orbitals, so it needs fewer iterations than the first.) OSZICAR gets a line per electronic step and a summary line (F=, E0=) per ionic step; OUTCAR gets a force block per ionic step; CONTCAR holds the positions of the last step.

These are the tags of Lab 1.

  • IBRION chooses the algorithm. Its default is a trap: setting only NSW selects molecular dynamics (IBRION = 0), not a relaxation.   • EDIFFG sets the stopping rule. A positive value stops when the energy changes by less than EDIFFG between ionic steps. A negative value stops when the force on every atom is below |EDIFFG|, so EDIFFG = -0.02 asks for forces below 0.02 eV/Å. A force criterion is the more direct test that a structure is at a minimum; the right threshold depends on the research question.
  • ISIF = 2 moves the atoms and keeps the cell fixed. Relaxing the cell shape and volume comes in .

Does a relaxation find the most stable structure?

No: it stops at the nearest point where the forces vanish, usually a local minimum.

Try the three starting points. From 0.35 Å the relaxation stops in the shallow minimum on the right, although the deeper one is on the left. From −0.30 Å it finds the deep one. From 0.06 Å it stops at once: the force there is already below 0.01 eV/Å, on top of the barrier.

An optimizer only ever follows the local slope, so it cannot know what lies beyond the next hill. A relaxation therefore stops at a stationary point near where it starts, usually a local minimum. If symmetry keeps the forces exactly at zero, it can also stop on a saddle point; a phonon calculation tells the two apart (). Whether a lower structure exists elsewhere is a different question, the subject of crystal structure prediction, which is how the carbon frameworks of Lecture 0 were found.

Which approximations have we already made?

At least three physical approximations come before any choice of exchange–correlation functional.

The Born–Oppenheimer approximation is not the exchange–correlation approximation. PBE, SCAN, DFT+U and HSE are later choices.

Before you choose any functional, a VASP relaxation already rests on at least three physical approximations. The third is easy to forget. A relaxed structure is the static one: the nuclei sit at the bottom of the energy surface, without thermal motion and without the zero-point motion that quantum mechanics requires even at absolute zero.

How big is the zero-point energy? Each vibrational mode of frequency \(\omega\) carries at least \(\tfrac12\hbar\omega\). In silicon every vibrational quantum is at most 64.5 meV, so each mode contributes at most 32 meV. An O–H bond stretch has a quantum of about 0.46 eV, so its zero-point energy is about 0.23 eV, enough to shift reaction energies and barriers that involve hydrogen. That is why nuclear quantum effects are largest for light atoms. Most simulations still treat the nuclei classically; phonon calculations add the zero-point energy back afterwards ().

Keep them apart

PBE, SCAN, DFT+U and HSE are choices made inside the electronic problem (, , , ). The approximations in this table come before all of them.

Why “half the way back”? The forces on the two atoms are equal and opposite, so the optimizer moves each one by the same amount in opposite directions and the midpoint between them stays put. The difference between the two positions returns to (0.25, 0.25, 0.25), to within the force tolerance, but the atoms themselves end near (0.01, 0, 0) and (0.26, 0.25, 0.25). Moving both atoms together costs no energy, so nothing pulls the pair back to the origin.

Why are the two forces equal and opposite? Moving both atoms together leaves the energy unchanged, so the sum of the slopes, \(\partial E/\partial\mathbf R_1 + \partial E/\partial\mathbf R_2\), must be zero: the forces cancel. In any calculation without external fields, the forces on all atoms add up to zero, which is a quick check on your OUTCAR.

Which misconceptions should you drop now?

Five beliefs about relaxations that this lecture should replace. Click a card to turn it over.

“The atoms know where to go.”

VASP computes forces from the energy model; the optimizer uses them to update the positions. Nothing more.

“Relaxed means globally stable.”

A relaxation stops near where it starts, usually in a local minimum, sometimes on a saddle point held by symmetry.

“Small forces mean the calculation is correct.”

Small forces mean the structure is stationary for the chosen model and settings. The model itself can still be wrong.

“The functional is the first approximation.”

Before any functional: no spin–orbit coupling, Born–Oppenheimer, and classical nuclei.

“If VASP converged, the physics is solved.”

Convergence means the stopping criteria were met. It does not mean the result is physically right.

Which particles and interactions does the full Hamiltonian contain?

Nuclei and electrons. Two kinetic energies, plus nucleus–nucleus and electron–electron repulsion and electron–nucleus attraction.

Why can the nuclei be clamped, and what does that neglect?

They are at least 1836 times heavier, so they move slowly. It neglects nonadiabatic couplings, small unless electronic states come close in energy.

Why does a relaxation solve the electrons again at every step?

Moving the nuclei changes the clamped-nuclei Hamiltonian, so the ground state, energy and forces must be recomputed. The forces, −∂E/∂R, set the next move.

No spin–orbit coupling (scalar-relativistic potentials), Born–Oppenheimer, and classical nuclei without zero-point motion.

What should you remember?

A relaxation asks one question again and again: does moving the nuclei lower the energy?

  • A material is nuclei and electrons; the Hamiltonian has two kinetic terms and three Coulomb terms, and electron–electron repulsion makes it many-body.
  • Born–Oppenheimer: nuclei at least 1836 times heavier can be clamped, so a geometry maps to an energy, \(\{\mathbf R_I\} \to E(\{\mathbf R_I\})\).
  • Forces are \(\mathbf F_I = -\partial E/\partial \mathbf R_I\). A relaxation follows them to the nearest stationary point, solving the electrons again at every step.
  • No spin–orbit coupling, Born–Oppenheimer and classical nuclei come before any choice of functional.
