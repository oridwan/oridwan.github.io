What is a wavefunction?

A function ψ(x) whose squared magnitude, |ψ|², is a probability density.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 23 / 18

The state of a quantum system is described by its wavefunction, ψ\psiψ. For one particle in three dimensions, ∣ψ(x,y,z)∣2 dx dy dz|\psi(x, y, z)|^2\,dx\,dy\,dz∣ψ(x,y,z)∣2dxdydz is the probability of finding the particle in the small volume dx dy dzdx\,dy\,dzdxdydz around the point (x,y,z)(x, y, z)(x,y,z). So ψ\psiψ itself is not a probability. It can be negative, and in general it is a complex number, ψ=u+iv\psi = u + ivψ=u+iv, whose squared magnitude ∣ψ∣2=ψ∗ψ=u2+v2|\psi|^2 = \psi^*\psi = u^2 + v^2∣ψ∣2=ψ∗ψ=u2+v2 is never negative. That squared magnitude is the probability density. In one dimension, the probability of finding the particle between aaa and bbb is

P(a≤x≤b)=∫ab∣ψ(x)∣2 dx.P(a \le x \le b) = \int_a^b |\psi(x)|^2\, dx .P(a≤x≤b)=∫ab​∣ψ(x)∣2dx.

The particle must be somewhere, so the probability over all space is one: ∫∣ψ∣2 dx=1\int |\psi|^2\, dx = 1∫∣ψ∣2dx=1. A wavefunction that satisfies this is normalized. Any solution can be normalized by dividing it by the square root of ∫∣ψ∣2dx\int|\psi|^2dx∫∣ψ∣2dx.

In the figure, the second state ψ2\psi_2ψ2​ is negative in the right half of the box, but ∣ψ2∣2|\psi_2|^2∣ψ2​∣2 is not: the overall sign of ψ\psiψ has no meaning on its own. Multiplying ψ\psiψ by −1-1−1, or by any eiθe^{i\theta}eiθ, changes no probability. Relative signs matter only when states are added together, as a later slide shows.

Most functions are changed by an operator into a different function. A few special ones come back as themselves, multiplied by a constant. They are the eigenfunctions of that operator, and the constants are its eigenvalues. "Eigen" is German for "own": the function's own value.

The plane wave eikxe^{ikx}eikx is an eigenfunction of momentum with eigenvalue ℏk\hbar kℏk: a state of definite momentum. The function sin⁡kx\sin kxsinkx is not, because differentiating it gives a cosine. It is, however, a sum of two plane waves, sin⁡kx=(eikx−e−ikx)/2i\sin kx = (e^{ikx} - e^{-ikx})/2isinkx=(eikx−e−ikx)/2i, with momenta +ℏk+\hbar k+ℏk and −ℏk-\hbar k−ℏk; a later slide shows what measuring the momentum of such a sum gives. Notice that sin⁡kx\sin kxsinkx is an eigenfunction of the kinetic-energy operator, with eigenvalue ℏ2k2/2m\hbar^2k^2/2mℏ2k2/2m: both of its plane waves have the same kinetic energy. Keep the plane wave in mind; in Lecture 17 VASP's whole basis is made of them.

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is an eigenvalue equation for the energy operator. It does not ask for the wavefunction; it asks for the special states whose energy is definite, and for their energies. They are called stationary states for a reason. Under the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, an energy eigenstate only acquires a rotating phase, ψn(x) e−iEnt/ℏ\psi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}ψn​(x)e−iEn​t/ℏ, so its probability density ∣ψn∣2|\psi_n|^2∣ψn​∣2 never changes. An electron in a stationary state of an atom does not move around in any measurable way.

There are usually many solutions, ordered by energy. The lowest is the ground state, the state DFT is built to describe; the others are excited states. The equation also shows where kinetic energy comes from: the second derivative is large where ψ\psiψ bends sharply, so a wavefunction squeezed into a small region, or one with many wiggles, has high kinetic energy. On the next slide, boundary conditions decide which solutions exist.

Why does energy come in steps?

Only standing waves that vanish at both walls are allowed, so only certain energies are.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 27 / 18

Put a particle between two infinitely high walls a distance LLL apart. Inside, V=0V = 0V=0, and the Schrödinger equation reads −ℏ22mψ′′=Eψ-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' = E\psi−2mℏ2​ψ′′=Eψ.   • Normalizing, ∫0LA2sin⁡2(nπx/L) dx=A2L/2=1\int_0^L A^2\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,dx = A^2L/2 = 1∫0L​A2sin2(nπx/L)dx=A2L/2=1, gives A=2/LA = \sqrt{2/L}A=2/L​.

What if the state is not an eigenstate?

Each measurement still gives one eigenvalue; the coefficients decide how often.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 28 / 18

Because the Schrödinger equation is linear, any sum of solutions is also a valid state. Any state can be written as a superposition of eigenstates, ψ=∑ncnψn\psi = \sum_n c_n \psi_nψ=∑n​cn​ψn​, and such a state has no definite energy. Yet a measurement of the energy always gives one of the eigenvalues, never anything in between. Which one is a matter of chance: eigenvalue EnE_nEn​ turns up with probability Pn=∣cn∣2P_n = |c_n|^2Pn​=∣cn​∣2. For a normalized ψ\psiψ these probabilities add up to one, ∑n∣cn∣2=1\sum_n |c_n|^2 = 1∑n​∣cn​∣2=1.

Try it: set c1c_1c1​ and c2c_2c2​ and press Measure once a few times. Every result is E1E_1E1​ or 4E14E_14E1​, never the average. Press Measure 100 times and the bars approach ∣c1∣2|c_1|^2∣c1​∣2 and ∣c2∣2|c_2|^2∣c2​∣2.

Notice also that the probability density of the superposition is not just the sum of the two densities. For real coefficients,

∣c1ψ1+c2ψ2∣2=c12ψ12+c22ψ22+2c1c2 ψ1ψ2.|c_1\psi_1 + c_2\psi_2|^2 = c_1^2\psi_1^2 + c_2^2\psi_2^2 + 2c_1c_2\,\psi_1\psi_2 .∣c1​ψ1​+c2​ψ2​∣2=c12​ψ12​+c22​ψ22​+2c1​c2​ψ1​ψ2​.

The cross term is interference. In the box, ψ1ψ2\psi_1\psi_2ψ1​ψ2​ is positive in the left half and negative in the right half, so with c1c2>0c_1c_2 > 0c1​c2​>0 the particle is more likely on the left, and flipping the sign of c2c_2c2​ moves it to the right. That is why the relative sign, or phase, of the parts of ψ\psiψ matters.

Not necessarily. In an eigenstate every measurement gives it; otherwise it is an average.

Why must observables be Hermitian?

Hermitian operators have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenstates that form a complete set.

  • Real eigenvaluesEvery measured value is a real number.
  • Orthogonal eigenstates∫ψm∗ ψn dx=δmn\int \psi_m^*\, \psi_n\, dx = \delta_{mn}∫ψm∗​ψn​dx=δmn​ once normalized: 1 if m=nm = nm=n, otherwise 0.
  • Expansionψ=∑ncnψn\psi = \sum_n c_n \psi_nψ=∑n​cn​ψn​ with cn=∫ψn∗ ψ dxc_n = \int \psi_n^*\, \psi\, dxcn​=∫ψn∗​ψdx.

An operator is Hermitian if it can be moved from one function to the other inside an integral, with a complex conjugate:

∫f∗ (A^g) dx=∫(A^f)∗ g dxfor all f,g.\int f^*\,(\hat A g)\,dx = \int (\hat A f)^*\, g\,dx \quad \text{for all } f, g.∫f∗(A^g)dx=∫(A^f)∗gdxfor all f,g.

Multiplication by a real function, such as xxx or V(x)V(x)V(x), obviously has this property. For p^=−iℏ d/dx\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dxp^​=−iℏd/dx it follows from integrating by parts, with the boundary terms vanishing because the wavefunctions go to zero far away; the factor −i-i−i is exactly what makes it work. Three consequences make the whole machinery of quantum mechanics work:

  • Real eigenvalues. Every possible measurement result is a real number.
  • Orthogonal eigenstates. Eigenstates with different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and degenerate ones can always be chosen orthogonal. The box states are an example: ∫0Lψ1ψ2 dx=0\int_0^L \psi_1\psi_2\,dx = 0∫0L​ψ1​ψ2​dx=0.
  • Completeness. The eigenstates form a complete set: any reasonable state can be expanded as ψ=∑ncnψn\psi = \sum_n c_n\psi_nψ=∑n​cn​ψn​. Orthonormality then makes each coefficient a simple overlap integral. Multiply the expansion by ψm∗\psi_m^*ψm∗​ and integrate: every term but one vanishes, leaving cm=∫ψm∗ψ dxc_m = \int\psi_m^*\psi\,dxcm​=∫ψm∗​ψdx.

NBANDS sets how many Kohn–Sham orbitals VASP computes at each k-point. It needs at least all the occupied ones plus one empty band, and its default adds a margin, which is a safe choice in most cases. Empty bands are needed because the iterative solver converges better with them, and because properties such as the band gap or optical spectra need the lowest unoccupied states. The number of electrons, NELECT, is taken from the POTCARs for a neutral cell: the valence electrons per atom, ZVAL, times the number of atoms of each species.

One orbital is not the electron density. The density adds up the occupied orbitals, each weighted by its occupation fnkf_{n\mathbf k}fnk​ (up to two electrons per orbital without spin polarization) and by the weight wkw_{\mathbf k}wk​ of its k-point; the weights add up to one. In OUTCAR, every k-point gets a block that starts with band No. and lists band energies and occupations.

Are VASP's eigenvalues measurable energies?

Not in general: they belong to an auxiliary problem, not to the real interacting electrons.

εHOMO=−I\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HOMO}} = -IεHOMO​=−I
  • The one exact link: with the exact functional, the highest occupied eigenvalue of a finite system is minus its first ionization energy III. The other eigenvalues have no exact meaning.
  • What they areEigenvalues of the Kohn–Sham Hamiltonian, a stand-in system of non-interacting electrons (Lecture 9).
  • Still usefulOften a fair, qualitative guide to single-particle energies.
  • Watch outThe band gap they give is not the measured gap (Lecture 30).

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 214 / 18

Everything in this lecture so far was exact quantum mechanics for one particle: each eigenvalue is a possible result of an energy measurement. The Kohn–Sham equations that VASP solves look the same, but their Hamiltonian describes a fictitious system of non-interacting electrons, constructed to have the same density as the real, interacting ones. Its eigenvalues are energies of that fictitious system.

Even with the exact exchange–correlation functional, only one of them has an exact physical meaning: the highest occupied eigenvalue of a finite system, an atom or molecule, equals minus its first ionization energy. In a periodic cell even this link cannot be used directly, because the eigenvalues have no vacuum level to be measured from. The others have no exact meaning, and in particular the Kohn–Sham gap differs from the true gap even with the exact functional, by a term called the derivative discontinuity (Lecture 30).

In practice, Kohn–Sham eigenvalues often approximate the energies of adding or removing an electron well enough to be qualitatively useful: band dispersions, orderings and densities of states usually look right, while band gaps come out too small. So read band structures with care until Lecture 29 and Lecture 30.

Which misconceptions should you drop now?

Five confusions about wavefunctions and eigenvalues. Click a card to turn it over.

“ψ is the probability of finding the particle.”

|ψ|² is a probability density. ψ itself can be negative or complex.

“The expectation value is the most likely result.”

It is an average. In a superposition it may lie between eigenvalues and never be measured.

“The sign of ψ means nothing.”

Alone it does not, but relative signs between added states change |ψ|² through interference.

“One orbital is the electron density.”

The density adds up all occupied orbitals, each weighted by its occupation.

“Every VASP eigenvalue is a measured energy.”

Kohn–Sham eigenvalues belong to an auxiliary system and need careful interpretation.

What should you remember?

State → wavefunction; observable → operator; possible result → eigenvalue.

  • |ψ|² is a probability density, and a normalized ψ integrates to one.
  • An eigenfunction comes back from its operator times a number; Ĥψ = Eψ asks for the states of definite energy.
  • A measurement gives an eigenvalue, with probability |c_n|²; the expectation value is the average and may never be measured.
  • In an orthonormal basis, Ĥψ = Eψ becomes Hc = Ec (in general Hc = ESc). VASP solves such a problem for NBANDS orbitals at every k-point.
  • Kohn–Sham eigenvalues are not, in general, measurable excitation energies.

Key terms

Operator
A rule that turns one function into another, written with a hat, A^\hat AA^. In quantum mechanics every observable has a linear, Hermitian operator: position multiplies by xxx, momentum is −iℏ d/dx-i\hbar\, d/dx−iℏd/dx, energy is the Hamiltonian.
Wavefunction
The function ψ\psiψ that describes the state of a quantum system. It is in general complex; its squared magnitude ∣ψ∣2|\psi|^2∣ψ∣2 is a probability density.
Probability density
∣ψ(r)∣2=ψ∗ψ|\psi(\mathbf r)|^2 = \psi^*\psi∣ψ(r)∣2=ψ∗ψ: the probability per unit volume of finding the particle at r\mathbf rr. Integrated over a region, it gives the probability of finding the particle there.
Normalization
The condition ∫∣ψ∣2 dτ=1\int |\psi|^2\, d\tau = 1∫∣ψ∣2dτ=1: the particle is certainly somewhere.
Hamiltonian
The operator for the total energy of a system. For a material it collects the kinetic energies of the electrons and nuclei and all their Coulomb interactions.
Eigenvalue equation
A^ψ=aψ\hat A\psi = a\psiA^ψ=aψ: the operator returns the same function multiplied by a number. ψ\psiψ is an eigenfunction (eigenstate) and aaa its eigenvalue. The Schrödinger equation H^ψ=Eψ\hat H\psi = E\psiH^ψ=Eψ is the eigenvalue equation of the energy.
Ground state
The lowest-energy state of a system. Ordinary DFT is a theory of the ground state; excited states need extensions of the theory or other methods.
Superposition
A sum of states, ψ=∑ncnψn\psi = \sum_n c_n\psi_nψ=∑n​cn​ψn​. Measuring an observable whose eigenstates are the ψn\psi_nψn​ gives eigenvalue ana_nan​ with probability ∣cn∣2|c_n|^2∣cn​∣2.
Expectation value
⟨A⟩=⟨ψ∣A^∣ψ⟩=∫ψ∗A^ψ dτ\langle A\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle = \int\psi^*\hat A\psi\,d\tau⟨A⟩=⟨ψ∣A^∣ψ⟩=∫ψ∗A^ψdτ for a normalized state: the average of many measurements, equal to ∑n∣cn∣2an\sum_n |c_n|^2 a_n∑n​∣cn​∣2an​. Only in an eigenstate is it guaranteed to be an eigenvalue, the result of every measurement.
Hermitian operator
An operator whose expectation values are always real. Its eigenvalues are real, its eigenstates can be chosen orthonormal, and they form a complete set, so any state can be expanded in them. Observables are Hermitian.
Orthonormal
A set of functions with ⟨ψm∣ψn⟩=δmn\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle = \delta_{mn}⟨ψm​∣ψn​⟩=δmn​: each normalized, and any two different ones orthogonal (overlap zero).
Basis set
A set of known functions ϕj\phi_jϕj​ in which an unknown state is expanded, ψ=∑jcjϕj\psi = \sum_j c_j\phi_jψ=∑j​cj​ϕj​. It turns H^ψ=Eψ\hat H\psi = E\psiH^ψ=Eψ into a matrix problem for the coefficients; a finite basis gives an approximate answer. VASP's basis is plane waves.
