DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 61 / 20

Lecture 5 ended with the Slater determinant, the simplest many-electron wavefunction that obeys the Pauli principle. This lecture asks which determinant is the best one, and answers with the Hartree–Fock method.

A DFT user needs Hartree–Fock for three reasons. Its self-consistent field is the loop that every VASP run performs. Its exchange term is the "exact exchange" that hybrid functionals mix in. And what it misses, the correlation energy, is exactly what a density functional has to supply. Helium is the worked example throughout: two electrons and one orbital, so every number can be computed by hand or in your browser. Energies are in hartree (Ha), the atomic unit of energy: 1 Ha = 27.211 eV.

Where does VASP's SCF loop come from?

From Hartree–Fock: the orbitals must produce the very field they move in.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 62 / 20

Every SCF loop solves the same chicken-and-egg problem. An electron's orbital depends on the field it moves in. That field depends on where the other electrons are, that is, on their orbitals. So you cannot compute the orbitals before you know the field, or the field before you know the orbitals.

The way out is the loop on the slide. Guess the orbitals; a common start is the orbitals of the bare nuclei, with the repulsion between electrons switched off. Build the field that the guess produces. Solve for the orbitals in that field. If the new orbitals differ from the guess, use them as the next guess and go around again. When input and output agree, the orbitals are self-consistent: they produce exactly the field in which they are the solutions.

Douglas Hartree introduced this self-consistent field in 1928 to compute atoms. In 1930 Vladimir Fock and John Slater made the wavefunction a Slater determinant, which adds the exchange term derived in this lecture; the result is Hartree–Fock theory. Kohn–Sham DFT keeps the loop and changes what goes into the second box. In VASP, each pass around the loop is one line of the OSZICAR file.

What is the best single Slater determinant?

The one with the lowest energy: Hartree–Fock applies the variational principle to the orbitals themselves.

EHF=min⁡{χa}[ ∑aha  +  ∑a<b(Jab−Kab)]E_{\mathrm{HF}} = \min_{\{\chi_a\}} \Big[\ \sum_{a} \eqterm{h}{h_a} \;+\; \sum_{a<b} \big(\eqterm{J}{J_{ab}} - \eqterm{K}{K_{ab}}\big) \Big]EHF​={χa​}min​[ a∑​ha​+a<b∑​(Jab​−Kab​)]
  • One-electron energy of spin-orbital aaa: its kinetic energy plus its attraction to the nuclei.
  • Coulomb integral: the classical repulsion between the charge clouds of orbitals aaa and bbb.
  • Exchange integral: a consequence of antisymmetry; nonzero only if aaa and bbb have the same spin.

Lecture 5 built the many-electron wavefunction as one Slater determinant of spin-orbitals χa(x)=ϕa(r)σa\chi_a(\mathbf x) = \phi_a(\mathbf r)\sigma_aχa​(x)=ϕa​(r)σa​, a spatial orbital times a spin function. Any such determinant is antisymmetric and normalized, so it is a legitimate trial wavefunction. By the variational principle of Lecture 3 its energy therefore lies at or above the exact ground-state energy, and the best determinant is the one with the lowest energy. What is new compared with Lecture 3 is what you vary: not a few numbers, but the orbitals themselves.

The energy of one determinant has the form on the slide. (The repulsion between the clamped nuclei is a constant and is left out.) Its three ingredients are

KabK_{ab}Kab​ has no classical counterpart; the next slide shows where it comes from. Both are positive, so exchange lowers the energy. Minimizing this energy shapes the orbitals. Because each electron now feels the repulsion of the others, the best orbitals are more spread out than those of the bare nucleus. The shielding of Lecture 4 comes out of the minimization rather than being put in by hand, as the helium example will show.

Where does the exchange term come from?

From antisymmetry: the determinant lets two electrons trade orbitals, which adds a second integral, and it survives only for equal spins.

Φ(x1,x2)=12[χ1(x1)χ2(x2)−χ2(x1)χ1(x2)]\Phi(\mathbf x_1, \mathbf x_2) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\big[\chi_1(\mathbf x_1)\chi_2(\mathbf x_2) - \chi_2(\mathbf x_1)\chi_1(\mathbf x_2)\big]Φ(x1​,x2​)=2​1​[χ1​(x1​)χ2​(x2​)−χ2​(x1​)χ1​(x2​)] ⟨Φ∣ 1r12 ∣Φ⟩=⟨χ1χ2∣ r12−1∣χ1χ2⟩  −  ⟨χ1χ2∣ r12−1∣χ2χ1⟩\Big\langle \Phi \Big|\, \frac{1}{r_{12}} \,\Big| \Phi \Big\rangle = \eqterm{J}{\big\langle \chi_1\chi_2 \big|\, r_{12}^{-1} \big| \chi_1\chi_2 \big\rangle} \;-\; \eqterm{K}{\big\langle \chi_1\chi_2 \big|\, r_{12}^{-1} \big| \chi_2\chi_1 \big\rangle}⟨Φ​r12​1​​Φ⟩=⟨χ1​χ2​​r12−1​​χ1​χ2​⟩−⟨χ1​χ2​​r12−1​​χ2​χ1​⟩
  • Direct term J12J_{12}J12​: each electron stays in its own orbital on both sides.
  • Exchange term K12K_{12}K12​: the electrons have traded orbitals on the right. The spin sum gives 1 for equal spins, 0 for opposite.

The repulsion. Inserting the determinant gives four terms, with a prefactor 12\tfrac1221​ from the normalization:

⟨Φ∣1r12∣Φ⟩=12[⟨12∣12⟩+⟨21∣21⟩−⟨12∣21⟩−⟨21∣12⟩].\Big\langle \Phi \Big| \frac{1}{r_{12}} \Big| \Phi \Big\rangle = \tfrac12\Big[\langle 12|12\rangle + \langle 21|21\rangle - \langle 12|21\rangle - \langle 21|12\rangle\Big].⟨Φ​r12​1​​Φ⟩=21​[⟨12∣12⟩+⟨21∣21⟩−⟨12∣21⟩−⟨21∣12⟩].

In the first integral each electron is in the same orbital on both sides: that is the Coulomb integral J12J_{12}J12​. In the second, the two electrons have swapped orbitals between the left and the right side. Such a term exists only because the wavefunction contains both orderings, and it is the exchange integral K12K_{12}K12​.

The spin. Write χa=ϕa(r)σa\chi_a = \phi_a(\mathbf r)\sigma_aχa​=ϕa​(r)σa​. In ⟨12∣21⟩\langle 12|21\rangle⟨12∣21⟩, electron 1 carries spin σ1\sigma_1σ1​ on the left and σ2\sigma_2σ2​ on the right, so its spin sum gives ⟨σ1∣σ2⟩\langle\sigma_1|\sigma_2\rangle⟨σ1​∣σ2​⟩; the same holds for electron 2. That product is 1 when the spins are equal and 0 when they are opposite. Two electrons of opposite spin therefore have no exchange term, and their repulsion is the classical J12J_{12}J12​ alone.

The rest. The one-electron part gives ⟨Φ∣h^(1)+h^(2)∣Φ⟩=h1+h2\langle\Phi|\hat h(1) + \hat h(2)|\Phi\rangle = h_1 + h_2⟨Φ∣h^(1)+h^(2)∣Φ⟩=h1​+h2​, because its cross terms contain the overlap ⟨χ1∣χ2⟩=0\langle\chi_1|\chi_2\rangle = 0⟨χ1​∣χ2​⟩=0. The total is E=h1+h2+J12−K12E = h_1 + h_2 + J_{12} - K_{12}E=h1​+h2​+J12​−K12​. For N electrons the same algebra applies to each pair separately, which gives the sum over pairs on the previous slide.

The minus sign has a physical meaning. Two electrons of the same spin repel each other less than the classical estimate, because antisymmetry makes their joint wavefunction vanish wherever they meet (the exchange hole of Lecture 5). On average they stay farther apart.

What field does each electron move in?

The nuclei plus the average Coulomb and exchange interaction with all the electrons: a mean field.

f^=h^+∑b(J^b−K^b),f^ χa=εa χa\hat f = \eqterm{h}{\hat h} + \sum_{b} \big( \eqterm{J}{\hat J_b} - \eqterm{K}{\hat K_b} \big), \qquad \hat f\, \chi_a = \eqterm{eps}{\varepsilon_a}\, \chi_af^​=h^+b∑​(J^b​−K^b​),f^​χa​=εa​χa​
  • One-electron part: kinetic energy and attraction to the nuclei.
  • Coulomb operator: multiplies by the electrostatic potential of orbital bbb's charge cloud.
  • Exchange operator: nonlocal, and only between orbitals of the same spin.
  • Orbital energy: the energy of one electron in orbital aaa in this field.

Minimizing the determinant energy over the orbitals, while keeping them orthonormal, leads to one-electron equations: the Hartree–Fock equations on the slide. The effective operator f^\hat ff^​ is the Fock operator. Its two new pieces act on an orbital χ\chiχ as follows:

J^b χ(x1)=[∫∣χb(x2)∣2r12 dx2]χ(x1),K^b χ(x1)=[∫χb∗(x2) χ(x2)r12 dx2]χb(x1).\hat J_b\,\chi(\mathbf x_1) = \Big[\int \frac{|\chi_b(\mathbf x_2)|^2}{r_{12}}\, d\mathbf x_2\Big]\chi(\mathbf x_1), \qquad \hat K_b\,\chi(\mathbf x_1) = \Big[\int \frac{\chi_b^*(\mathbf x_2)\,\chi(\mathbf x_2)}{r_{12}}\, d\mathbf x_2\Big]\chi_b(\mathbf x_1).J^b​χ(x1​)=[∫r12​∣χb​(x2​)∣2​dx2​]χ(x1​),K^b​χ(x1​)=[∫r12​χb∗​(x2​)χ(x2​)​dx2​]χb​(x1​).

J^b\hat J_bJ^b​ is an ordinary potential: it multiplies χ\chiχ by the electrostatic potential that the charge cloud of orbital b creates at r1\mathbf r_1r1​. K^b\hat K_bK^b​ is different in two ways. To evaluate K^bχ\hat K_b\chiK^b​χ at one point you need χ\chiχ at every point, because χ\chiχ sits inside the integral; an operator like this is called nonlocal. And the result is proportional to χb\chi_bχb​, not to χ\chiχ. Nonlocality is what makes exact exchange expensive in a plane-wave code (Lecture 27).

The picture is a mean field. Each electron feels the nuclei and the average charge distribution of the electrons, plus exchange, but not where the other electrons happen to be at a given instant. The orbital energy

εa=⟨χa∣f^∣χa⟩=ha+∑b(Jab−Kab)\varepsilon_a = \langle\chi_a|\hat f|\chi_a\rangle = h_a + \sum_b \big(J_{ab} - K_{ab}\big)εa​=⟨χa​∣f^​∣χa​⟩=ha​+b∑​(Jab​−Kab​)

is the energy of one electron in this field, including its repulsion with every other electron.

The sum runs over all occupied orbitals, including b=ab = ab=a. Acting on χa\chi_aχa​ itself, J^aχa=K^aχa\hat J_a\chi_a = \hat K_a\chi_aJ^a​χa​=K^a​χa​, so the self-term contributes nothing (next slide). Including it makes f^\hat ff^​ the same operator for every orbital. The occupied orbitals are then eigenfunctions of one Hermitian operator, and so automatically orthogonal.

How minimizing the energy gives the Hartree–Fock equations

Write the energy as E=∑a⟨a∣h^∣a⟩+12∑a,b(⟨ab∣ab⟩−⟨ab∣ba⟩)E = \sum_a \langle a|\hat h|a\rangle + \tfrac12\sum_{a,b}\big(\langle ab|ab\rangle - \langle ab|ba\rangle\big)E=∑a​⟨a∣h^∣a⟩+21​∑a,b​(⟨ab∣ab⟩−⟨ab∣ba⟩); the terms with a=ba = ba=b cancel, so this is the pair sum of the determinant energy. Change one orbital by a small amount, χa→χa+δχa\chi_a \to \chi_a + \delta\chi_aχa​→χa​+δχa​. Orbital a appears in both slots of the pair sum, which cancels the 12\tfrac1221​, and to first order

δE=⟨δχa∣f^∣χa⟩+complex conjugate.\delta E = \langle \delta\chi_a|\hat f|\chi_a\rangle + \text{complex conjugate}.δE=⟨δχa​∣f^​∣χa​⟩+complex conjugate.

The orbitals must stay orthonormal, so add the constraints with Lagrange multipliers λba\lambda_{ba}λba​ and minimize E−∑a,bλba(⟨χa∣χb⟩−δab)E - \sum_{a,b}\lambda_{ba}\big(\langle\chi_a|\chi_b\rangle - \delta_{ab}\big)E−∑a,b​λba​(⟨χa​∣χb​⟩−δab​). Setting the change to zero for every δχa\delta\chi_aδχa​ gives f^χa=∑bλbaχb\hat f\chi_a = \sum_b \lambda_{ba}\chi_bf^​χa​=∑b​λba​χb​, with a Hermitian matrix λ\lambdaλ.

Now use a freedom of the determinant. Replacing the occupied orbitals by any unitary combination of themselves multiplies the determinant by a phase and leaves the density, f^\hat ff^​ and E unchanged. Choose the combination that diagonalizes λ\lambdaλ. In that basis f^χa=εaχa\hat f\chi_a = \varepsilon_a\chi_af^​χa​=εa​χa​: the canonical Hartree–Fock equations on the slide, with the eigenvalues of λ\lambdaλ as orbital energies.

Does an electron repel itself in Hartree–Fock?

No: for every electron, the exchange term cancels its repulsion with its own charge exactly.

Jaa−Kaa=0J_{aa} - K_{aa} = 0Jaa​−Kaa​=0
  • Same orbital, same spin: the Coulomb and exchange integrals become the same integral.
  • Helium, Hartree energy of the total density2J=2.052J = 2.052J=2.05 Ha: as if each electron also repelled itself.
  • Exchange−J=−1.03-J = -1.03−J=−1.03 Ha, about −28 eV: removes the self-repulsion.
  • Approximate DFTThe cancellation is incomplete: the self-interaction error.

There are two ways to book-keep the repulsion between electrons, and comparing them shows what exchange does.

The pair sum in the determinant energy, ∑a<b(Jab−Kab)\sum_{a<b}(J_{ab} - K_{ab})∑a<b​(Jab​−Kab​), counts each pair of different electrons once and never an electron with itself. DFT books it differently. It starts from the classical electrostatic energy of the total density n(r)=∑a∣χa(r)∣2n(\mathbf r) = \sum_a |\chi_a(\mathbf r)|^2n(r)=∑a​∣χa​(r)∣2, the Hartree energy

U=12∬n(r1) n(r2)r12 dr1 dr2=12∑a,bJab.U = \frac12 \iint \frac{n(\mathbf r_1)\, n(\mathbf r_2)}{r_{12}}\, d\mathbf r_1\, d\mathbf r_2 = \frac12\sum_{a,b} J_{ab}.U=21​∬r12​n(r1​)n(r2​)​dr1​dr2​=21​a,b∑​Jab​.

This double sum includes the terms a=ba = ba=b: every electron repelling its own charge cloud. The Hartree–Fock exchange energy is Ex=−12∑a,bKabE_x = -\tfrac12\sum_{a,b}K_{ab}Ex​=−21​∑a,b​Kab​, again including a=ba = ba=b. Set b=ab = ab=a in the definitions of JabJ_{ab}Jab​ and KabK_{ab}Kab​, and they become the same integral, Kaa=JaaK_{aa} = J_{aa}Kaa​=Jaa​. The self-terms cancel one by one, and U+ExU + E_xU+Ex​ equals the pair sum exactly.

Helium makes this concrete. Both electrons occupy the same spatial orbital ϕ1s\phi_{1s}ϕ1s​, with opposite spins, and their repulsion is J=J1s,1s=1.026J = J_{1s,1s} = 1.026J=J1s,1s​=1.026 Ha (27.9 eV) in the converged calculation of the coming slides. The density is n=2∣ϕ1s∣2n = 2|\phi_{1s}|^2n=2∣ϕ1s​∣2, so the Hartree energy is U=12⋅2⋅2⋅J=2JU = \tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot J = 2JU=21​⋅2⋅2⋅J=2J. But helium has a single pair of electrons, whose repulsion is JJJ. The extra JJJ is self-interaction. The exchange energy is −12(K1s↑,1s↑+K1s↓,1s↓)=−J-\tfrac12(K_{1s\uparrow,1s\uparrow} + K_{1s\downarrow,1s\downarrow}) = -J−21​(K1s↑,1s↑​+K1s↓,1s↓​)=−J, which removes it exactly. The two electrons, having opposite spins, have no exchange term with each other.

Approximate exchange–correlation functionals replace ExE_xEx​ by an approximation that cancels the self-repulsion only partly. What remains is the self-interaction error, one of the weaknesses of DFT listed in Lecture 0 and treated in Lecture 10.

One orbital and two formulas: E = 2h + J and ε = h + J. Each freedom given to the orbital lowers the energy.

Hartree–Fock therefore recovers 98.6% of the energy and misses 0.042 Ha, or 1.14 eV. As a fraction that is small. But chemistry is about energy differences, and a typical covalent bond is worth only a few eV (about 5 eV in Lecture 5's estimate). Fluorine is a striking example. In the Hartree–Fock approximation the F₂ molecule lies higher in energy than two separate fluorine atoms, by more than 1 eV, so it should fall apart. The real molecule is bound by about 1.6 eV. All of its binding comes from correlation.

Orbital energies are by-products of the calculation, but in Hartree–Fock they have a physical meaning. Take one electron out of helium and leave the remaining orbital exactly as it was. The ion's energy is then just the one-electron energy hhh, so the energy needed to remove the electron is h−(2h+J)=−(h+J)=−εh - (2h + J) = -(h + J) = -\varepsilonh−(2h+J)=−(h+J)=−ε. The same algebra works for any occupied orbital of any atom, molecule or solid: −εa-\varepsilon_a−εa​ is the ionization energy from orbital a when all other orbitals are frozen. For helium, −ε1s=0.918-\varepsilon_{1s} = 0.918−ε1s​=0.918 Ha = 24.98 eV, against the exact 24.59 eV: 0.39 eV too high. That small error hides two larger ones.

  • Freezing the orbital overestimates the cost. In the real ion the remaining electron no longer feels the other's repulsion and contracts towards the nucleus, lowering the ion's energy. Recomputing the ion with a relaxed orbital (He⁺ has one electron, so its energy is exactly −2 Ha) gives −2 − (−2.8617) = 0.8617 Ha = 23.45 eV, 1.53 eV lower.
  • Missing correlation works the other way. Neutral helium has a correlation energy of −1.14 eV that the one-electron ion lacks, so the relaxed value is 1.14 eV too low.

Koopmans' estimate is good because these two errors, +1.53 eV and −1.14 eV, mostly cancel. The same idea gives electron affinities from empty orbitals, but there the two errors add rather than cancel, and the estimate is poor. Kohn–Sham orbital energies are different again. Apart from the highest occupied one, which equals minus the ionization energy for the exact functional, they have no such general meaning (Lecture 2).

What is the correlation energy?

The exact energy minus the Hartree–Fock energy: what a mean field cannot capture.

Ecorr=Eexact−EHF  ≤  0E_{\mathrm{corr}} = E_{\mathrm{exact}} - E_{\mathrm{HF}} \;\le\; 0Ecorr​=Eexact​−EHF​≤0
  • Never positive, by the variational principle; negative for two or more electrons.
  • Physical originElectrons avoid each other at every instant, not just on average.
  • SizeAbout 1% of the total energy, yet decisive for bond energies.
  • Correlated wavefunction methodsAdd more determinants: accurate, but their cost climbs steeply with size.

DFT & VASP Course · Lecture 613 / 20

The correlation energy is the difference between the exact energy and the Hartree–Fock energy for the same Hamiltonian. The Hartree–Fock determinant is a trial wavefunction, so EHF≥EexactE_{\mathrm{HF}} \ge E_{\mathrm{exact}}EHF​≥Eexact​, and EcorrE_{\mathrm{corr}}Ecorr​ is never positive. For helium it is −0.042 Ha.

What does a single determinant miss? Helium's Hartree–Fock wavefunction is ϕ(r1)ϕ(r2)\phi(\mathbf r_1)\phi(\mathbf r_2)ϕ(r1​)ϕ(r2​) times a spin function. The probability of finding electron 2 at a point does not depend on where electron 1 is: the two move independently, each in the average field of the other. Real electrons do not. When one is close to the nucleus, the other tends to be farther out (radial correlation), and the two prefer opposite sides of the nucleus (angular correlation). Exchange already keeps electrons of the same spin apart. Correlation is the further avoidance, for both spins, driven by the repulsion itself. Because they avoid each other better, real electrons repel each other less, and the energy is lower.

Wavefunction methods recover correlation by going beyond one determinant. They mix in determinants in which electrons are promoted to empty orbitals, which lets the wavefunction depend on where the other electrons are. The exact answer in a given basis, full configuration interaction, uses all such determinants. Their number grows exponentially with the number of electrons: the exponential wall of Lecture 0. Practical methods truncate the expansion, at a cost that still grows steeply with system size, as the fifth power for MP2 and the seventh for CCSD(T), the "gold standard" of quantum chemistry. That limits them to molecules and small cells. DFT instead approximates correlation by a functional of the density, at the cost of a mean-field calculation.

From the outside, Hartree–Fock and Kohn–Sham DFT look alike. Both describe the electrons by orbitals in a single determinant, both solve one-electron equations with an effective operator, and both iterate to self-consistency. They differ in what goes into the effective operator, and in what the determinant means.

In Hartree–Fock the determinant is the approximation to the true wavefunction; exchange is treated exactly, and correlation is missing. In Kohn–Sham theory the determinant belongs to an auxiliary system of non-interacting electrons, constructed to have the same density as the real ones; it is not meant to approximate the true wavefunction. Exchange and correlation are combined into one functional of the density, Exc[n]E_{xc}[n]Exc​[n]. In principle it contains all the correlation energy that Hartree–Fock misses; in practice it must be approximated.

Hybrid functionals sit in between. They take a fraction of exact Hartree–Fock exchange, 25% in PBE0, and the rest of exchange and all of correlation from a density functional. Part II builds Kohn–Sham theory from the ground up, starting with Lecture 7.

Which misconceptions should you drop now?

Six beliefs about Hartree–Fock and the SCF loop. Click a card to turn it over.

“Hartree–Fock ignores electron repulsion.”

It includes the average repulsion and exact exchange. It misses only correlation.

“Self-consistency means the answer is exact.”

It means the orbitals reproduce their own field. The basis and the method can still be in error.

“1% of the energy is negligible.”

For helium it is 1.14 eV, and without it F₂ is unbound. Chemistry lives in such differences.

“The total energy is the sum of the orbital energies.”

That sum counts each pair's repulsion twice. Subtract it once (VASP: DENC, XCENC).

“HF and Kohn–Sham orbital energies mean the same.”

HF ones estimate ionization energies (Koopmans); Kohn–Sham ones in general do not.

“DFT is Hartree–Fock with a correction.”

It shares the SCF machinery, but its determinant describes an auxiliary system, and one functional gives exchange and correlation.

What should you remember?

The best single determinant, found self-consistently, misses only correlation, and that still matters.

  • Hartree–Fock minimizes the energy of one determinant. Antisymmetry adds exchange between equal spins; each electron moves in the mean field of the Fock operator.

Key terms

Self-consistent field (SCF)
The iterative loop in which the density determines the potential, the potential determines the orbitals, and the orbitals give a new density, repeated until input and output agree.
Spin-orbital
A one-electron function of position and spin, χ(x)=ϕ(r)σ(s)\chi(\mathbf x) = \phi(\mathbf r)\sigma(s)χ(x)=ϕ(r)σ(s): a spatial orbital times a spin function, α (up) or β (down).
Variational principle
For any allowed trial function ϕ\phiϕ, ⟨ϕ∣H^∣ϕ⟩/⟨ϕ∣ϕ⟩≥E0\langle\phi|\hat H|\phi\rangle/\langle\phi|\phi\rangle \ge E_0⟨ϕ∣H^∣ϕ⟩/⟨ϕ∣ϕ⟩≥E0​, the exact ground-state energy; equality only for the ground state. Lowering the energy over a trial family therefore improves the estimate of the ground-state energy.
Fock operator
The effective one-electron operator of Hartree–Fock theory: kinetic energy, attraction to the nuclei, and the Coulomb and exchange operators built from the occupied orbitals. In a basis it is the Fock matrix, and the equations read FC = SCε.
Mean field
An approximation in which each electron feels the average field of all the others rather than their instantaneous positions. Hartree–Fock and Kohn–Sham theory are both mean-field methods.
Self-interaction error
The spurious interaction of an electron with its own charge that remains in approximate functionals, because their exchange–correlation energy does not exactly cancel the electron's Coulomb energy with itself.
Basis set
A set of known functions ϕj\phi_jϕj​ in which an unknown state is expanded, ψ=∑jcjϕj\psi = \sum_j c_j\phi_jψ=∑j​cj​ϕj​. It turns H^ψ=Eψ\hat H\psi = E\psiH^ψ=Eψ into a matrix problem for the coefficients; a finite basis gives an approximate answer. VASP's basis is plane waves.
Koopmans' theorem
In Hartree–Fock theory, minus the energy of an occupied orbital approximates the energy needed to remove an electron from it (an ionization energy), with the other orbitals frozen.
Correlation energy
Ecorr=Eexact−EHFE_{\mathrm{corr}} = E_{\mathrm{exact}} - E_{\mathrm{HF}}Ecorr​=Eexact​−EHF​, negative by definition: the energy of electrons avoiding each other beyond the mean field. About 1% of the total energy, but decisive for chemistry.
Electron correlation
The way electrons of either spin avoid each other because they repel, beyond what a single Slater determinant describes. The correlation energy is the difference between the exact and the Hartree–Fock energy.
